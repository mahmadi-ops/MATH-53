    1. Two Expansions, One Determinant.

    Find \(\det \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp 1 \amp 3 \\ 2 \amp 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\) by expanding along the second row, and again by expanding along the first column.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Along row \(2\text{.}\) The signs on row \(2\) alternate \(-, +, -\text{,}\) so by Definition 6.4
    \begin{align*} \det A \amp = -2 \begin{vmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{vmatrix} + 1 \begin{vmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp 1 \end{vmatrix} - 3 \begin{vmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ 2 \amp 1 \end{vmatrix}\\ \amp = -2(1) + 1(-1) - 3(-3)\\ \amp = -2 - 1 + 9 = 6. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Along column \(1\text{.}\) The signs down column \(1\) are \(+, -, +\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \det A \amp = 1 \begin{vmatrix} 1 \amp 3 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{vmatrix} - 2 \begin{vmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{vmatrix} + 2 \begin{vmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 3 \end{vmatrix}\\ \amp = 1(-2) - 2(1) + 2(5)\\ \amp = -2 - 2 + 10 = 6. \end{align*}
    🔗
    The two agree, as they must: every row and every column expansion of a matrix gives the same number.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. ★ Computing a Minor and a Cofactor.

    Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 3 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Compute \(\operatorname{minor}(A)_{32}\) and \(C_{32}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    For a \(3 \times 3\) matrix the signs alternate as
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} + \amp - \amp + \\ - \amp + \amp - \\ + \amp - \amp + \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    so position \((3,2)\) carries a minus sign.
    🔗
    Deleting row \(3\) and column \(2\) leaves
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{minor}(A)_{32} = \begin{vmatrix} 2 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp -1 \end{vmatrix} = -2 - 0 = -2, \end{equation*}
    and therefore
    \begin{equation*} C_{32} = (-1)^{3+2} \operatorname{minor}(A)_{32} = (-1)^5(-2) = 2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. ★ Two Expansions, One Answer.

    For the matrix \(A\) of Exercise 10.6.2, compute \(\det A\) by expanding along the first row and again along the second column.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Along the first row.
    \begin{align*} \det A \amp = (2)(-1)^{1+1}\begin{vmatrix} 3 \amp -1 \\ 2 \amp 1 \end{vmatrix} + (1)(-1)^{1+2}\begin{vmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{vmatrix}\\ \amp \qquad + (0)(-1)^{1+3}\begin{vmatrix} 1 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp 2 \end{vmatrix}\\ \amp = 2(5) - 1(1) + 0 = 9. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Along the second column.
    \begin{align*} \det A \amp = (1)(-1)^{1+2}\begin{vmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{vmatrix} + (3)(-1)^{2+2}\begin{vmatrix} 2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{vmatrix}\\ \amp \qquad + (2)(-1)^{3+2}\begin{vmatrix} 2 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp -1 \end{vmatrix}\\ \amp = -1 + 3(2) - 2(-2) = -1 + 6 + 4 = 9. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Both give \(9\text{,}\) as every row and column expansion must.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. ★ A \(4 \times 4\) Determinant.

    Find \(\det A\) for
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 2 \amp 1 \amp -1 \\ 4 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2 \amp 0 \\ 2 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Choose a row or column with many zeros. The fourth row is \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) so expand along it:
    \begin{align*} \det A \amp = (2)(-1)^{4+1} \begin{vmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 3 \\ 1 \amp 2 \amp 0 \end{vmatrix}\\ \amp \qquad + (1)(-1)^{4+4} \begin{vmatrix} 0 \amp 2 \amp 1 \\ 4 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2 \end{vmatrix}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Each remaining \(3 \times 3\) determinant has a row with two zeros. For the first, expand along its second row:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{vmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 3 \\ 1 \amp 2 \amp 0 \end{vmatrix} = (3)(-1)^{2+3}\begin{vmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 2 \end{vmatrix} = -3(3) = -9. \end{equation*}
    For the second, again along its second row:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{vmatrix} 0 \amp 2 \amp 1 \\ 4 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2 \end{vmatrix} = (4)(-1)^{2+1}\begin{vmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 2 \end{vmatrix} = -4(3) = -12. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \det A = (2)(-1)(-9) + (1)(1)(-12) = 18 - 12 = 6. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    5. A Determinant by Row Reduction.

    Find \(\det \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp 3 \amp 2 \\ -4 \amp 1 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}\) using row operations.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Adding a multiple of one row to another leaves the determinant unchanged (Theorem 6.11), so we may reduce freely as long as we only use that one type of operation:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp 3 \amp 2 \\ -4 \amp 1 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow[4r_1 + r_3]{-2r_1 + r_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 9 \amp 6 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \xrightarrow{9r_2 + r_3} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 6 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The last matrix is upper triangular, so by Theorem 6.9 its determinant is the product of the diagonal entries:
    \begin{equation*} \det A = 1 \cdot (-1) \cdot 6 = -6. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6. ★ How Do Elementary Row Operations Affect Determinants?

    Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp 0 \amp 2 \\ 0 \amp -2 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 0 \amp 4 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Compute \(\det A\text{,}\) then compute the determinants of the matrices obtained from \(A\) by (i) interchanging rows \(1\) and \(3\text{,}\) (ii) multiplying row \(1\) by \(5\text{,}\) and (iii) adding \(3\) times row \(3\) to row \(1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Row \(2\) has two zeros, so expand along it throughout:
    \begin{equation*} \det A = (-2)(-1)^{2+2}\begin{vmatrix} 3 \amp 2 \\ 1 \amp 4 \end{vmatrix} = -2(12 - 2) = -20. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (i) \(B_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 4 \\ 0 \amp -2 \amp 0 \\ 3 \amp 0 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) and
    \begin{equation*} \det B_1 = (-2)\begin{vmatrix} 1 \amp 4 \\ 3 \amp 2 \end{vmatrix} = -2(2 - 12) = 20 = -\det A. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (ii) \(B_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 15 \amp 0 \amp 10 \\ 0 \amp -2 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 0 \amp 4 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) and
    \begin{equation*} \det B_2 = (-2)\begin{vmatrix} 15 \amp 10 \\ 1 \amp 4 \end{vmatrix} = -2(60 - 10) = -100 = 5 \det A. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (iii) \(B_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 6 \amp 0 \amp 14 \\ 0 \amp -2 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 0 \amp 4 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) and
    \begin{equation*} \det B_3 = (-2)\begin{vmatrix} 6 \amp 14 \\ 1 \amp 4 \end{vmatrix} = -2(24 - 14) = -20 = \det A. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    So an interchange flips the sign, scaling a row scales the determinant by the same factor, and adding a multiple of one row to another leaves the determinant alone — exactly Theorem 6.11.
    🔗

    7. ★ Computing a Determinant by Row Reduction.

    Without using cofactor expansion, find \(\det A\) for \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp 1 \amp 2 \\ 1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp 4 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Reduce to triangular form, recording the effect of each operation:
    \begin{equation*} A \xrightarrow{r_1 \leftrightarrow r_2} B_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \\ 3 \amp 1 \amp 2 \\ 2 \amp 4 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \xrightarrow[-2r_1 + r_3]{-3r_1 + r_2} B_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp -5 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp -2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The first step was an interchange, which flips the sign; the last two added multiples of one row to another, which changes nothing. \(B_2\) is upper triangular, so by Theorem 6.9 its determinant is the product of its diagonal entries:
    \begin{equation*} \det A = -\det B_1 = -\det B_2 = -(1)(-5)(-2) = -10. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    8. ★ Scaling a \(4 \times 4\) Matrix.

    Suppose \(A\) is \(4 \times 4\) with \(\det A = -3\text{.}\) Find \(\det(2A)\) and \(\det(-A)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Multiplying \(A\) by a scalar \(k\) multiplies every one of its \(4\) rows by \(k\text{,}\) and each row scaling multiplies the determinant by \(k\text{.}\) So by Theorem 6.14,
    \begin{equation*} \det(kA) = k^4 \det A. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \det(2A) = 2^4(-3) = -48, \qquad \det(-A) = (-1)^4(-3) = -3. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The second answer is worth pausing on: for a matrix of even size, negating it leaves the determinant unchanged. For odd size the sign would flip.
    🔗
    🔗

    9. True or False?

    Decide whether each statement is true or false, and justify your answer.
    1. If \(A\) is a \(3 \times 3\) matrix with \(\det A = 0\text{,}\) then one column of \(A\) must be a multiple of another.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If two columns of a square matrix are equal, then its determinant is \(0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. For \(n \times n\) matrices, \(\det(A + B) = \det A + \det B\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. For an \(n \times n\) matrix, \(\det(3A) = 3 \det A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. If \(A^{-1}\) exists then \(\det(A^{-1}) = (\det A)^{-1}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    6. If \(B\) is obtained by multiplying a single row of \(A\) by \(4\text{,}\) then \(\det B = 4 \det A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    7. For an \(n \times n\) matrix, \(\det(-A) = (-1)^n \det A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    8. If \(A\) is a real \(n \times n\) matrix, then \(\det(A^T A) \geq 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) False. A zero determinant means the columns are linearly dependent, which is weaker than one being a multiple of another. Take
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 3 \\ 4 \amp 5 \amp 6 \\ 7 \amp 8 \amp 9 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    whose determinant is \(0\) because the third column is \(2\) times the second minus the first, yet no column is a multiple of any other.
    🔗
    (b) True. Subtracting one of the equal columns from the other produces a zero column without changing the determinant, and a matrix with a zero column has determinant \(0\) (expand along that column).
    🔗
    (c) False. Determinants are not additive. Take \(A = B = I_2\text{:}\) then \(\det(A + B) = \det(2I_2) = 4\text{,}\) while \(\det A + \det B = 1 + 1 = 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (d) False for \(n \gt 1\text{.}\) Scaling every row by \(3\) multiplies the determinant by \(3\) once per row, so \(\det(3A) = 3^n \det A\) (Theorem 6.14). With \(A = I_2\text{,}\) \(\det(3A) = 9 \neq 3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (e) True. From \(AA^{-1} = I\) and Theorem 6.19, \(\det A \cdot \det(A^{-1}) = 1\text{,}\) so \(\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det A\text{.}\) (Note \(\det A \neq 0\) since \(A\) is invertible.)
    🔗
    (f) True. This is the single-row version of (d): scaling one row by \(4\) multiplies the determinant by \(4\text{.}\) Expand along that row and every term picks up a factor of \(4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (g) True. \(-A\) is \(A\) with all \(n\) rows scaled by \(-1\text{,}\) giving a factor of \((-1)^n\text{.}\) This is (d) with the scalar \(-1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (h) True. Using Theorem 6.21 and then Theorem 6.19,
    \begin{equation*} \det(A^T A) = \det(A^T)\det(A) = (\det A)^2 \geq 0, \end{equation*}
    since \(\det A\) is a real number.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    10. Does This Matrix Have an Inverse?

    Determine whether \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 2 \amp 1 \\ 3 \amp 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\) has an inverse by deciding whether its determinant is nonzero.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Expand along the first column, whose middle entry is \(0\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \det A \amp = 1 \begin{vmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{vmatrix} + 3 \begin{vmatrix} 2 \amp 0 \\ 2 \amp 1 \end{vmatrix}\\ \amp = 1(2 - 1) + 3(2 - 0) = 1 + 6 = 7. \end{align*}
    (The signs down column \(1\) are \(+, -, +\text{,}\) and the third-row cofactor contributes \(+3 \cdot \det \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 0 \\ 2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\))
    🔗
    Since \(\det A = 7 \neq 0\text{,}\) \(A\) is invertible by Theorem 6.16. Its inverse can then be found with Algorithm 4.40; it is
    \begin{equation*} A^{-1} = \frac{1}{7}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -2 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp 1 \amp -1 \\ -6 \amp 5 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    11. Parameters and Invertibility.

    1. For which real \(c\) is \(A = \begin{bmatrix} c \amp 1 \amp 2 \\ 1 \amp 0 \amp c \\ 2 \amp c \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\) invertible?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. For which real \(a\) is \(A = \begin{bmatrix} a+1 \amp 0 \amp -3 \\ 0 \amp a+1 \amp 0 \\ 4 \amp 0 \amp a-6 \end{bmatrix}\) invertible?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) Expand along the second row, which contains a \(0\text{.}\) The signs on row \(2\) are \(-, +, -\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \det A \amp = -1 \begin{vmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ c \amp 0 \end{vmatrix} - c \begin{vmatrix} c \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp c \end{vmatrix}\\ \amp = -1(0 - 2c) - c(c^2 - 2)\\ \amp = 2c - c^3 + 2c = 4c - c^3\\ \amp = c(2 - c)(2 + c). \end{align*}
    This is zero exactly when \(c = 0\text{,}\) \(c = 2\) or \(c = -2\text{,}\) so \(A\) is invertible for every value of \(c\) except those three.
    🔗
    (b) Expand along the second row, whose only nonzero entry is \(a+1\) in position \((2,2)\) with sign \(+\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \det A \amp = (a+1)\begin{vmatrix} a+1 \amp -3 \\ 4 \amp a-6 \end{vmatrix}\\ \amp = (a+1)\bigl[(a+1)(a-6) + 12\bigr]\\ \amp = (a+1)(a^2 - 5a + 6)\\ \amp = (a+1)(a-2)(a-3). \end{align*}
    So \(A\) is invertible for every \(a\) except \(a = -1\text{,}\) \(a = 2\) and \(a = 3\text{.}\)
    🔗

    12. Determinants of Complicated Expressions.

    1. Let \(A\text{,}\) \(B\text{,}\) \(C\) be \(4 \times 4\) with \(\det A = 4\text{,}\) \(\det B = 2\text{,}\) \(\det C = -1\text{.}\) Find \(\det\left( \tfrac{1}{2} A^{-1} B^T C^3 A^2 \right)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Let \(A\text{,}\) \(B\text{,}\) \(C\) be \(2 \times 2\) with \(\det A = -1\text{,}\) \(\det B = 2\text{,}\) \(\det C = -6\text{.}\) Find \(\det\left( A^{-1} C^2 (3B^T)^{-1} A^2 \right)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Everything follows from three rules: \(\det(MN) = \det M \det N\text{,}\) \(\det(M^T) = \det M\text{,}\) and \(\det(kM) = k^n \det M\) for \(n \times n\) matrices.
    🔗
    (a) Here \(n = 4\text{,}\) so the scalar \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) contributes \(\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^4 = \tfrac{1}{16}\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \det\left( \tfrac{1}{2}A^{-1}B^T C^3 A^2 \right) \amp = \tfrac{1}{16} \cdot \frac{1}{\det A} \cdot \det B \cdot (\det C)^3 \cdot (\det A)^2\\ \amp = \tfrac{1}{16} \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 2 \cdot (-1)^3 \cdot 16\\ \amp = -\tfrac{1}{2}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    (b) Here \(n = 2\text{,}\) and \(\det(3B^T) = 3^2 \det B = 9 \cdot 2 = 18\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \det\left( A^{-1}C^2(3B^T)^{-1}A^2 \right) \amp = \frac{1}{\det A} \cdot (\det C)^2 \cdot \frac{1}{18} \cdot (\det A)^2\\ \amp = \det A \cdot (\det C)^2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{18}\\ \amp = (-1)(36)\left(\tfrac{1}{18}\right) = -2. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Notice that in (b) the factors \(\frac{1}{\det A}\) and \((\det A)^2\) combine to a single \(\det A\text{:}\) since determinants are ordinary numbers, the order of the matrix factors never affects the answer.

    13. ★ True or False?

    If \(A\) and \(B\) are square matrices and \(B\) is not invertible, then \(AB\) is not invertible.
    🔗
    Solution.
    True. We prove the contrapositive: if \(AB\) is invertible, then \(B\) is invertible.
    🔗
    Suppose \(AB\) is invertible, so there is a matrix \(C\) with \(C(AB) = I\text{.}\) Regrouping,
    \begin{equation*} (CA)B = I, \end{equation*}
    and by condition (8) of Theorem 4.57, \(B\) is invertible with \(B^{-1} = CA\text{.}\)
    🔗
    A determinant argument is even shorter: \(B\) not invertible gives \(\det B = 0\text{,}\) so \(\det(AB) = \det A \det B = 0\) by Theorem 6.19, and \(AB\) is not invertible by Theorem 6.16.

    14. ★ True or False?

    For any elementary matrices \(E\text{,}\) \(F\) and any square matrix \(M\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \det(EFM) = \det(E) \det(F) \det(M). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    True. By Example 6.18 we already know that \(\det(EN) = \det(E)\det(N)\) for every elementary \(E\) and every square \(N\text{.}\) Apply that fact twice, first with \(N = FM\) and then with \(N = M\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \det(EFM) \amp = \det\bigl( E(FM) \bigr)\\ \amp = \det(E) \det(FM)\\ \amp = \det(E) \det(F) \det(M). \end{align*}
    🔗
    The same induction extends to any number of elementary factors: \(\det(E_1 E_2 \cdots E_k M) = \det(E_1)\cdots\det(E_k)\det(M)\text{.}\) Since every invertible matrix is a product of elementary matrices, this is exactly how Theorem 6.19 gets proved in general.

    15. ★ A Determinant of a Complicated Product.

    Suppose \(A\text{,}\) \(B\text{,}\) \(C\) are \(3 \times 3\) matrices with \(\det A = 2\text{,}\) \(\det B = -3\text{,}\) \(\det C = 1\text{.}\) Find
    \begin{equation*} \det \left( 3 A^{-1} B^2 (C^T) A B^{-1} \right). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Split the product and pull the scalar out, remembering that a scalar on a \(3 \times 3\) matrix contributes its cube:
    \begin{align*} \det \left( 3 A^{-1} B^2 C^T A B^{-1} \right) \amp = 3^3 \cdot \frac{1}{\det A} \cdot (\det B)^2\\ \amp \qquad \cdot \det C \cdot \det A \cdot \frac{1}{\det B}\\ \amp = 27 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 9 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 \cdot \left( -\tfrac{1}{3} \right)\\ \amp = -81. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Each rule earned its keep: the \(3^3\) comes from Theorem 6.14 with \(n = 3\text{;}\) the product splits by Theorem 6.19; the reciprocals come from Corollary 6.20; and \(\det(C^T) = \det C\) by Theorem 6.21.
    🔗
    Notice that \(\frac{1}{\det A}\) and \(\det A\) cancel, as do one factor of \(\det B\) against \(\frac{1}{\det B}\text{.}\) Determinants are ordinary numbers, so the order of the matrix factors never matters.
