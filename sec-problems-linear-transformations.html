    Problems for Transformations and Linear Transformations (Section 3.1).
    🔗

    1. A Transformation from Two of Its Values.

    Let \(T : \R^3 \to \R^4\) be a linear transformation with
    \begin{equation*} T \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad T \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 2 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Find \(T \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ -1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    A linear transformation is determined on any vector we can write as a combination of vectors whose images we know, by Theorem 3.10. So look for \(a\) and \(b\) with
    \begin{equation*} a \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + b \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ -1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The second entry gives \(-a = -1\text{,}\) so \(a = 1\text{;}\) the first then gives \(1 + b = -1\text{,}\) so \(b = -2\text{.}\) The third entry checks: \(2(1) - (-2) = 4\text{.}\) \(\checkmark\)
    🔗
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} T \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ -1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = 1 \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} - 2 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 2 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -4 \\ -3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. When Two Values Are Not Enough.

    Let \(T : \R^3 \to \R^3\) be a linear transformation with
    \begin{equation*} T \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad T \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Find \(T \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(T \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Both questions come down to writing the target as a combination of the two vectors we know. Row reduce with both right-hand sides at once:
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{cc|cc} 0 \amp 1 \amp 2 \amp 2 \\ -1 \amp 1 \amp 1 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp -2 \amp -2 \amp -3 \end{array} \right] \longrightarrow \left[ \begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2 \amp 2 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp -1 \end{array} \right]. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The first vector. Its column of the reduced matrix ends in \(0\text{,}\) so the system is consistent and
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} = 1 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + 2 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Applying \(T\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} T \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + 2 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The second vector. Its column ends in \(-1\text{,}\) so that system reads \(0 = -1\) and is inconsistent: \([2\ 1\ {-3}]^T\) is not in \(\operatorname{Span}\left\{ [0\ {-1}\ 2]^T, [1\ 1\ {-2}]^T \right\}\text{.}\) The two given values place no constraint on \(T\) there, so \(T \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}\) cannot be determined from the information given.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. The Matrix from Two Values.

    A linear transformation \(T : \R^2 \to \R^2\) satisfies
    \begin{equation*} T \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix}, \qquad T \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} c \\ d \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Find the matrix of \(T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The matrix of \(T\) has columns \(T(\vec{e}_1)\) and \(T(\vec{e}_2)\text{,}\) so we need to express the standard basis vectors in terms of the two given ones.
    🔗
    Subtracting,
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \vec{e}_1, \end{equation*}
    and, looking for \(\alpha, \beta\) with \(\alpha (2,-1) + \beta (1,-1) = (0,1)\text{,}\) the equations \(2\alpha + \beta = 0\) and \(-\alpha - \beta = 1\) give \(\alpha = 1\text{,}\) \(\beta = -2\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} - 2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \vec{e}_2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Applying \(T\) to both,
    \begin{equation*} T(\vec{e}_1) = \begin{bmatrix} a - c \\ b - d \end{bmatrix}, \qquad T(\vec{e}_2) = \begin{bmatrix} a - 2c \\ b - 2d \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    so the matrix of \(T\) is
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} a - c \amp a - 2c \\ b - d \amp b - 2d \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Composing Two Transformations.

    Let \(S, T : \R^2 \to \R^2\) be linear transformations with
    \begin{equation*} S \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad S \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad T \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Find \((S \circ T) \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    By definition \((S \circ T)(\vec{v}) = S\bigl(T(\vec{v})\bigr)\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} (S \circ T) \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = S \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    We are not told \(S(1,-1)\) directly, so write it in terms of the two vectors whose \(S\)-images we do know. Looking for \(a, b\) with
    \begin{equation*} a \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + b \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    the first entry gives \(a = 1\) and the second gives \(1 - 2b = -1\text{,}\) so \(b = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} S \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. ★ A Transformation from \(\R^3\) to \(\R^4\).

    Let \(T : \R^3 \to \R^4\) be defined by
    \begin{equation*} T \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x - z \\ x + y \\ 2y \\ y + z \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Identify the domain and codomain, and compute the image of \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    By Definition 3.1, the domain is \(\R^3\) and the codomain is \(\R^4\text{:}\) the rule takes a vector with three entries and returns one with four.
    🔗
    Substituting \(x = 2\text{,}\) \(y = 1\text{,}\) \(z = -1\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} T \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 - (-1) \\ 2 + 1 \\ 2(1) \\ 1 + (-1) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6. ★ The Previous Transformation Is a Matrix Transformation.

    Show that the transformation of Exercise 10.3.5 is a matrix transformation, and find its matrix.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Split the output according to the three input variables:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} x - z \\ x + y \\ 2y \\ y + z \end{bmatrix} = x \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + y \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + z \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    That is exactly a matrix-vector product, by Definition 2.13:
    \begin{equation*} T(\vec{x}) = A\vec{x}, \qquad A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The matrix is \(4 \times 3\text{,}\) as it must be to send \(\R^3\) to \(\R^4\text{.}\) Checking the earlier value, \(A[2\ 1\ {-1}]^T = (3,3,2,0)\text{.}\) \(\checkmark\)
    🔗

    7. ★ Transformation by Matrix Multiplication.

    Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 0 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp -3 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Describe the effect of \(A\) on a general vector, and compute the image of \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ -4 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(A\) is \(2 \times 3\text{,}\) so it sends vectors of \(\R^3\) to vectors of \(\R^2\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 0 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp -3 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}_{2 \times 3} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix}_{3 \times 1} = \begin{bmatrix} 2x + z \\ x - 3y \end{bmatrix}_{2 \times 1}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For the given vector,
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 0 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp -3 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ -4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 - 4 \\ 1 - 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ -5 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Note that \(y\) never affects the first output and \(z\) never affects the second, because of the two zeros in \(A\text{.}\)

    8. ★ Determining a Transformation from Two Values.

    Let \(T : \R^3 \to \R^4\) be linear with
    \begin{equation*} T \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad T \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \\ -1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Find \(T \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 4 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First write the target as a linear combination of the two known inputs. Solving \(a_1\vec{x}_1 + a_2\vec{x}_2 = (-1,4,-3)\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{cc|c} 1 \amp 2 \amp -1 \\ 2 \amp 1 \amp 4 \\ 1 \amp 3 \amp -3 \end{array} \right] \longrightarrow \left[ \begin{array}{cc|c} 1 \amp 2 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp -3 \amp 6 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -2 \end{array} \right] \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \longrightarrow \left[ \begin{array}{cc|c} 1 \amp 0 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -2 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{array} \right], \end{equation*}
    so \(a_1 = 3\) and \(a_2 = -2\text{;}\) that is,
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 4 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix} = 3\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - 2\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Now apply property (3) of Theorem 3.10:
    \begin{align*} T \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 4 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix} \amp = 3\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} - 2\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \\ -1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}\\ \amp = \begin{bmatrix} 9 \\ -4 \\ 5 \\ -11 \end{bmatrix}. \end{align*}

    9. ★ Transformations That Are Not Matrix Transformations.

    Give two transformations \(\R^2 \to \R^2\) that are not matrix transformations, and explain why each fails.
    🔗
    Solution.
    A translation. Let \(T(\vec{x}) = \vec{x} + \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} T(\vec{0}) = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \neq \vec{0}, \end{equation*}
    whereas every linear transformation satisfies \(T(\vec{0}) = \vec{0}\) by Theorem 3.10. So \(T\) is not linear, and by Theorem 3.12 it cannot be a matrix transformation. Directly: \(A\vec{0} = \vec{0}\) for every matrix \(A\text{,}\) so no \(A\) can reproduce this \(T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    A product of coordinates. Let \(T \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} xy \\ y \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) This one does fix \(\vec{0}\text{,}\) so we must look further. Scalar multiplication fails:
    \begin{equation*} T \left( 2 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right) = T \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 2 \, T \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = 2 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    and these differ. Equivalently, if \(A = \begin{bmatrix} a \amp b \\ c \amp d \end{bmatrix}\) worked we would need \(ax + by = xy\) for all \(x, y\text{,}\) which no constants \(a, b\) can achieve.
    🔗
    The lesson is that fixing \(\vec{0}\) is necessary but not sufficient: the second example passes that test and still fails to be linear.

    10. ★ True or False?

    Let \(T(\vec{x}) = A\vec{x}\text{,}\) where \(A\) is \(m \times n\text{,}\) and suppose \(T\) is onto. Which of the following must be true?
    1. For every \(\vec{b} \in \R^m\text{,}\) the equation \(T(\vec{x}) = \vec{b}\) has exactly one solution.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(A\vec{x} = \vec{0}\) has only the trivial solution.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The columns of \(A\) span \(\R^m\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    A single example settles the first two. Take \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(T : \R^3 \to \R^2\text{,}\) which is onto because \(A\) has a pivot in every row.
    🔗
    (I) False. The third column has no pivot, so \(x_3\) is free and every consistent system has infinitely many solutions. Being onto guarantees at least one solution for each \(\vec{b}\text{,}\) never exactly one.
    🔗
    (II) False. With the same \(A\text{,}\) \(A\vec{x} = \vec{0}\) has the nontrivial solution \(\vec{x} = (-1,-1,1)\text{,}\) since \(A(-1,-1,1) = (-1+1,\ -1+1) = \vec{0}\text{.}\) That statement characterizes one-to-one, not onto, by Theorem 3.16.
    🔗
    (III) True. By Definition 2.13, \(A\vec{x}\) is a linear combination of the columns of \(A\text{,}\) so the range of \(T\) is exactly \(\operatorname{Span}\{\vec{A}_1, \ldots, \vec{A}_n\}\text{.}\) Saying \(T\) is onto says that this span is all of \(\R^m\text{.}\)

    11. ★ Which Transformation Is an Injection?

    1. Reflection in the line \(y = x\) in \(\R^2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Projection into the \(y\)-axis in \(\R^2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(T \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ x + y \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (A) An injection. The matrix is \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) and \(A\vec{x} = \vec{0}\) forces \(y = 0\) and \(x = 0\text{.}\) Only the trivial solution, so \(T\) is one-to-one by Theorem 3.16.
    🔗
    (B) Not an injection. Here \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) and \(A\vec{x} = \vec{0}\) holds for every \(\vec{x}\) on the \(x\)-axis. For instance \(T(1,0) = T(0,0) = \vec{0}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (C) An injection. The matrix is
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    which has a pivot in each of its two columns, so \(A\vec{x} = \vec{0}\) has only the trivial solution. Directly: \(A\vec{x} = \vec{0}\) forces \(x = 0\) from the first row and \(y = 0\) from the second.
    🔗

    12. ★ A One-to-One Transformation, Seen Geometrically.

    Let \(T : \R^2 \to \R^3\) be induced by \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Is \(T\) one-to-one?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Row reduce \(A\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{r_1 \leftrightarrow r_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 2 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \xrightarrow{-2r_1 + r_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{-3r_2 + r_3} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    There is a pivot in every column, so \(A\vec{x} = \vec{0}\) has only the trivial solution and \(T\) is one-to-one by Theorem 3.16.
    🔗
    Note that \(T\) cannot also be onto: \(A\) is tall, and by Remark 3.33 a tall matrix is never onto. Its range is a plane inside \(\R^3\text{,}\) computed in Exercise 10.3.17.

    13. ★ A Transformation That Is Not One-to-One, Seen Geometrically.

    Let \(T : \R^3 \to \R^2\) be induced by \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Is \(T\) one-to-one?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The third column has no pivot, so \(z\) is free. Solving \(A\vec{x} = \vec{0}\text{,}\) the second row gives \(y = -z\) and the first gives \(x = -2y = 2z\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x} = z \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad z \in \R. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Taking \(z = 1\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} T \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 - 2 + 0 \\ 0 - 1 + 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = T \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    so two different inputs share an output and \(T\) is not one-to-one.
    🔗
    There was never a chance: \(A\) is wide, and by Remark 3.24 a wide matrix is never one-to-one.

    14. ★ A Transformation That Is Not Onto.

    Let \(T : \R^2 \to \R^2\) be defined by \(T \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ a - b \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Is \(T\) onto?

    Solution.

    🔗
    In matrix terms \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) which has only one pivot and hence no pivot in its first row. The range is the \(y\)-axis, a line inside the two-dimensional codomain.

    15. ★ Which Transformation Is a Surjection?

    1. \(T : \R^3 \to \R^2\) with \(T(\vec{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} x + y \\ y - z \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(T(\vec{x}) = A\vec{x}\) with \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (I) A surjection. The matrix has the images of the standard basis vectors as columns:
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    It has a pivot in each of its two rows, so the augmented matrix of \(A\vec{x} = \vec{b}\) can never acquire a pivot in its last column, and the system is consistent for every \(\vec{b}\text{.}\) Explicitly, \(\vec{x} = (b_1 - b_2,\ b_2,\ 0)\) works.
    🔗
    (II) Not a surjection. Row reducing \(\left[ \begin{array}{cc|c} 1 \amp 0 \amp b_1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp b_2 \\ 1 \amp 1 \amp b_3 \end{array} \right]\) by \(-r_1 + r_3\) and then \(-r_2 + r_3\) gives last row \([\,0\ 0 \mid b_3 - b_1 - b_2\,]\text{.}\) So the system is inconsistent whenever \(b_3 \neq b_1 + b_2\) — for instance \(\vec{b} = (0,0,1)\text{.}\) The range is the plane \(b_3 = b_1 + b_2\text{,}\) not all of \(\R^3\text{.}\)

    16. ★ An Onto Transformation, Seen Geometrically.

    Let \(T : \R^3 \to \R^2\) be induced by \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Is \(T\) onto?
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(A\) is already in row-echelon form with pivots in columns \(1\) and \(2\) — one in each of its two rows. So the augmented matrix of \(A\vec{x} = \vec{b}\) can never have a pivot in its last column, and the system is consistent for every \(\vec{b} \in \R^2\text{.}\) Hence \(T\) is onto.
    🔗
    Concretely, given \(\vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) take \(\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 - 2b_2 \\ b_2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} T(\vec{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} (b_1 - 2b_2) + 2b_2 \\ b_2 + 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \end{bmatrix}. \checkmark \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This is the same matrix as in Exercise 10.3.13, so \(T\) is onto but not one-to-one: each \(\vec{b}\) has a whole line of preimages.

    17. ★ A Transformation That Is Not Onto, Seen Geometrically.

    Let \(T : \R^2 \to \R^3\) be induced by \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Is \(T\) onto? Describe the range.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Every output is a linear combination of the two columns:
    \begin{equation*} T \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = x \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + y \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2x + y \\ x \\ 3y \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Writing \(\vec{b} = (b_1, b_2, b_3)\) for an output, the second and third entries give \(x = b_2\) and \(y = \tfrac{1}{3}b_3\text{,}\) and then the first entry must satisfy \(b_1 = 2b_2 + \tfrac{1}{3}b_3\text{.}\) Clearing the fraction, the range is the plane
    \begin{equation*} 3b_1 - 6b_2 - b_3 = 0 \end{equation*}
    inside \(\R^3\text{.}\) Both columns satisfy it: \(6 - 6 - 0 = 0\) and \(3 - 0 - 3 = 0\text{.}\) \(\checkmark\)
    🔗
    So \(T\) is not onto — for instance \(\vec{b} = (1,0,0)\) gives \(3 \neq 0\) and has no preimage. As Remark 3.33 predicts, a tall matrix never is.

    18. ★ A Transformation That Is Neither One-to-One Nor Onto.

    Let \(T : \R^3 \to \R^2\) be induced by \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp -1 \amp 3 \\ -4 \amp 2 \amp -6 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Show that \(T\) is neither one-to-one nor onto.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Row reducing,
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp -1 \amp 3 \\ -4 \amp 2 \amp -6 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{2r_1 + r_2} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp -1 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    since the second row is \(-2\) times the first. There is a single pivot.
    🔗
    Not one-to-one. Columns \(2\) and \(3\) carry no pivot, so \(A\vec{x} = \vec{0}\) has two free variables. Its solution set is the whole plane \(2x - y + 3z = 0\) in \(\R^3\text{,}\) so there are plenty of nontrivial solutions.
    🔗
    Not onto. There is no pivot in the second row. Every column is a multiple of \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) so the range is \(\operatorname{Span}\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} \right\}\text{,}\) a line in \(\R^2\text{.}\) The vector \(\vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\) is not on that line, since \((1,1) = t(1,-2)\) would need \(t = 1\) and \(t = -\tfrac{1}{2}\) at once. So \(T(\vec{x}) = \vec{b}\) has no solution.

    19. ★ Projection into the \(xz\)-Plane Is Neither.

    Let \(P : \R^3 \to \R^3\) be projection into the \(xz\)-plane,
    \begin{equation*} P \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ 0 \\ z \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Show that \(P\) is neither one-to-one nor onto.
    🔗
    Solution.

    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Not one-to-one. \(A\vec{x} = \vec{0}\) reads \(x = 0\text{,}\) \(0 = 0\text{,}\) \(z = 0\text{,}\) so \(y\) is free and the solution set is the whole \(y\)-axis, \(\vec{x} = t\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Concretely \(P(0,1,0) = P(0,2,0) = \vec{0}\text{:}\) the projection forgets \(y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Not onto. Every output has second entry \(0\text{,}\) so the range is \(\operatorname{Span}\{\vec{e}_1, \vec{e}_3\}\text{,}\) the \(xz\)-plane, which is smaller than the codomain \(\R^3\text{.}\) The vector \(\vec{b} = \vec{e}_2\) has no preimage, since the second equation of \(A\vec{x} = \vec{b}\) reads \(0 = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Both failures show up at once in \(\det A = 0\text{,}\) as Remark 3.42 says they must for a square matrix.

    20. ★ A Transformation That Is Both One-to-One and Onto.

    Let \(T : \R^2 \to \R^2\) be induced by \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Show that \(T\) is both one-to-one and onto.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Row reducing,
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{-r_2 + r_1} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{-2r_1 + r_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    so there is a pivot in every column (\(T\) is one-to-one) and in every row (\(T\) is onto).
    🔗
    In fact \(\det A = 3 - 2 = 1 \neq 0\text{,}\) so \(A\) is invertible with
    \begin{equation*} A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \\ -2 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    and every \(\vec{b}\) has the unique preimage \(\vec{x} = A^{-1}\vec{b}\text{.}\) Checking,
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \\ -2 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \checkmark \end{equation*}

    21. ★ Reflection Is Both One-to-One and Onto.

    Let \(Q_m : \R^2 \to \R^2\) be reflection in the line \(y = mx\text{.}\) Show that \(Q_m\) is both one-to-one and onto, for every \(m\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    By Example 3.50,
    \begin{equation*} Q_m = \frac{1}{m^2+1}\begin{bmatrix} 1 - m^2 \amp 2m \\ 2m \amp m^2 - 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Taking determinants, and remembering that a scalar factor of \(\frac{1}{m^2+1}\) on a \(2 \times 2\) matrix contributes its square,
    \begin{align*} \det Q_m \amp = \frac{1}{(m^2+1)^2}\left[ (1-m^2)(m^2-1) - 4m^2 \right]\\ \amp = \frac{-\left[ (1-m^2)^2 + 4m^2 \right]}{(m^2+1)^2}\\ \amp = \frac{-(m^2+1)^2}{(m^2+1)^2} = -1, \end{align*}
    using \((1-m^2)^2 + 4m^2 = 1 + 2m^2 + m^4 = (m^2+1)^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Since \(\det Q_m = -1 \neq 0\) for every \(m\text{,}\) the matrix is invertible. By Theorem 4.57, \(Q_m\vec{x} = \vec{0}\) has only the trivial solution, so \(Q_m\) is one-to-one, and its columns span \(\R^2\text{,}\) so it is onto.
    🔗
    The geometry says it faster: reflecting twice returns every vector to where it started, so \(Q_m^2 = I\) and \(Q_m^{-1} = Q_m\text{.}\) Every \(\vec{b}\) has exactly one preimage, namely \(Q_m(\vec{b})\) itself. Note also that \(\det Q_m = -1\) rather than \(+1\text{:}\) a reflection reverses orientation, where a rotation preserves it.

    24. A Reflection Followed by a Rotation.

    Find the matrix of the linear transformation obtained by reflecting in the \(y\)-axis and then rotating through \(\pi\) radians. What familiar transformation is the result?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Reflection in the \(y\)-axis sends \(\vec{e}_1 \mapsto -\vec{e}_1\) and fixes \(\vec{e}_2\text{,}\) so its matrix is \(M_y = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Rotation through \(\pi\) reverses every vector, so by Definition 3.43 its matrix is \(R_\pi = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Doing the reflection first means multiplying by \(M_y\) first, so the composite matrix is \(R_\pi M_y\text{,}\) with \(M_y\) on the right:
    \begin{equation*} R_\pi M_y = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    That is reflection in the \(x\)-axis (Example 3.48) — a reflection, not a rotation. Composing two reflections gives a rotation, but here we composed a reflection with a rotation.

    30. ★ Completing the Reflection in \(y = mx\).

    1. Complete the exercise left open in Example 3.50: show that \(Q_m(\vec{e}_2) = \dfrac{1}{m^2+1}\begin{bmatrix} 2m \\ m^2 - 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Write down the matrix of reflection in the line \(y = 2x\) and check it on two vectors.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) Reflecting in a line at angle \(\theta\) can be done in three steps: rotate the line down onto the \(x\)-axis, reflect in the \(x\)-axis, rotate back. Writing \(c = \cos\theta\) and \(s = \sin\theta\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} Q_m = R_\theta \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix} R_{-\theta}. \end{equation*}
    Now
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} c \amp s \\ -s \amp c \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} c \amp s \\ s \amp -c \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    and multiplying on the left by \(R_\theta\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} Q_m = \begin{bmatrix} c \amp -s \\ s \amp c \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} c \amp s \\ s \amp -c \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} c^2 - s^2 \amp 2cs \\ 2cs \amp s^2 - c^2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Substituting \(c = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{m^2+1}}\) and \(s = \dfrac{m}{\sqrt{m^2+1}}\) gives \(c^2 - s^2 = \dfrac{1-m^2}{m^2+1}\) and \(2cs = \dfrac{2m}{m^2+1}\text{,}\) so the second column is
    \begin{equation*} Q_m(\vec{e}_2) = \begin{bmatrix} 2cs \\ s^2 - c^2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{m^2+1}\begin{bmatrix} 2m \\ m^2 - 1 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    as claimed, and the full matrix is the one displayed in the example.
    🔗
    (b) With \(m = 2\) we get \(m^2 + 1 = 5\) and
    \begin{equation*} Q_2 = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} -3 \amp 4 \\ 4 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The direction along the line is \((1,2)\text{,}\) and it should be fixed: \(\frac{1}{5}(-3 + 8,\ 4 + 6) = (1,2)\text{.}\) \(\checkmark\) The perpendicular direction \((2,-1)\) should be reversed: \(\frac{1}{5}(-6 - 4,\ 8 - 3) = (-2,1)\text{.}\) \(\checkmark\)

    31. ★ Two Reflections Make a Rotation.

    Show that reflecting in the \(x\)-axis and then in the \(y\)-axis gives reflection through the origin, and identify that transformation as a rotation.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The two matrices are \(M_x = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(M_y = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Doing \(M_x\) first means it stands on the right:
    \begin{equation*} M_y M_x = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    That matrix sends \(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}\) to \(\begin{bmatrix} -x \\ -y \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) which is reflection through the origin. By Example 3.52 it is also \(R_\pi\text{,}\) rotation through half a turn.
    🔗
    Note that the two reflections commute here, \(M_yM_x = M_xM_y\text{,}\) which is special: reflections in two lines meeting at angle \(\alpha\) compose to a rotation through \(2\alpha\text{,}\) and the order matters unless \(\alpha\) is a right angle.

    32. ★ Projection into the \(y\)-Axis.

    Find the matrix of projection into the \(y\)-axis, and explain why this transformation is not one-to-one.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Projection into the \(y\)-axis sends \(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}\) to \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ y \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Since \(T(\vec{e}_1) = \vec{0}\) and \(T(\vec{e}_2) = \vec{e}_2\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    It is not one-to-one because the first coordinate is discarded: every vector on a given horizontal line has the same image. For instance
    \begin{equation*} T \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = T \begin{bmatrix} -5 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    two different inputs with one output. Equivalently \(A\vec{x} = \vec{0}\) has the nontrivial solution \(\vec{x} = \vec{e}_1\text{.}\)

    33. ★ Horizontal Shear.

    A horizontal shear fixes \(\vec{e}_1\) and sends \(\vec{e}_2\) to \(\begin{bmatrix} k \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Find its matrix, and describe its effect on the unit square when \(k = 3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The columns of the matrix are the images of the standard basis vectors, so
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp k \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x + ky \\ y \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Each point slides horizontally by an amount proportional to its \(y\)-coordinate, and the horizontal direction is left alone.
    🔗
    With \(k = 3\text{,}\) the corners of the unit square go to
    \begin{equation*} (0,0) \mapsto (0,0), \quad (1,0) \mapsto (1,0), \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} (0,1) \mapsto (3,1), \quad (1,1) \mapsto (4,1). \end{equation*}
    The square becomes a parallelogram with the same base and height, hence the same area — the horizontal counterpart of Figure 3.56. Consistently, \(\det A = 1\text{.}\)
