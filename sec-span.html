    Section 2.4 Span of a Set of Vectors

    Several figures in this section and the next are interactive. Each shows a matrix transformation \(T(\vec{x}) = A\vec{x}\) whose columns are the vectors under discussion, so the teal region in the right pane is exactly their span. Click and drag the head of the input vector \(\vec{x}\) or the output vector \(\vec{b}\text{;}\) drag inside a three-dimensional pane to rotate it. The violet object in the left pane is the solution set of \(T(\vec{x}) = \vec{b}\text{,}\) which is a single point exactly when the vectors are linearly independent.
    🔗

    Definition 2.25. Span.

    Let \(\{ \vec{u}_1, \vec{u}_2, \ldots, \vec{u}_k \}\) be a set of vectors in \(\R^n\text{.}\) Then the collection of all linear combinations of these vectors is called the span of these vectors, written \(\operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{u}_1, \vec{u}_2, \ldots, \vec{u}_k \}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.26. Describing a Span.

    Describe the span of the vectors \(\vec{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Note that any linear combination of \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\) is of the form
    \begin{equation*} a_1 \vec{u} + a_2 \vec{v} = a_1 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + a_2 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ a_1 + 2a_2 \\ 2a_1 + 3a_2 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    which can be thought of as a vector in the \(yz\)-plane.
    🔗
    Also, consider an arbitrary vector \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix}\) in the \(yz\)-plane. We can write any such vector as a linear combination of \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\text{.}\) Solving
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = x_1 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + x_2 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    amounts to row reducing
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{cc|c} 1 \amp 2 \amp y \\ 2 \amp 3 \amp z \end{array} \right] \longrightarrow \left[ \begin{array}{cc|c} 1 \amp 2 \amp y \\ 0 \amp -1 \amp z - 2y \end{array} \right] \longrightarrow \left[ \begin{array}{cc|c} 1 \amp 0 \amp -3y + 2z \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2y - z \end{array} \right], \end{equation*}
    giving \(x_1 = -3y + 2z\) and \(x_2 = 2y - z\text{.}\) That is,
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = (-3y + 2z) \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + (2y - z) \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Hence \(\operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{u}, \vec{v} \}\) is the \(yz\)-plane.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.28. What Happens When We Add a Third Vector?

    Consider the previous example, where the span of \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\) was the \(yz\)-plane. Suppose we add another vector \(\vec{w}\text{,}\) and consider the span of \(\vec{u}\text{,}\) \(\vec{v}\text{,}\) and \(\vec{w}\text{.}\) What would happen to the span?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Case (I): \(\vec{w}\) is also a vector in the \(yz\)-plane. For example, consider \(\vec{w} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Then \(\vec{w}\) is already in \(\operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{u}, \vec{v} \}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \vec{w} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = -5\vec{u} + 3\vec{v} = -5 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + 3 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Hence \(\operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{u}, \vec{v}, \vec{w} \} = \operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{u}, \vec{v} \}\) — adding \(\vec{w}\) gained us nothing.
    🔗
    Case (II): \(\vec{w}\) is not in the \(yz\)-plane. For example, \(\vec{w} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Notice that any vector in \(\R^3\) can be written as a linear combination of \(\vec{u}\text{,}\) \(\vec{v}\text{,}\) and \(\vec{w}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = x_1 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + x_2 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} + x_3 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    where
    \begin{equation*} x_1 = 2z - 3y - 3x, \qquad x_2 = 2x + 2y - z, \qquad x_3 = x. \end{equation*}
    Hence \(\operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{u}, \vec{v}, \vec{w} \} = \R^3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The moral: a new vector enlarges the span exactly when it is not already in the span. This is the idea that the next section makes precise.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.4.1 Seeing a Span

    The examples above were settled by row reduction. The same facts can be seen directly: the span of two vectors in \(\R^3\) that are not multiples of one another is a plane through the origin, and a vector lies in that span exactly when it lies on that plane.
    🔗

    Remark 2.32.

    The plane in these two figures has a compact description. A vector is perpendicular to both \(\vec{v}_1\) and \(\vec{v}_2\) exactly when it is perpendicular to the whole plane, and \(\vec{n} = (2,-2,1)\) is such a vector. So
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Span}\{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2\} = \left\{ (x,y,z) \in \R^3 \; : \; 2x - 2y + z = 0 \right\}. \end{equation*}
    Substituting confirms the two figures: \(\vec{w} = (2,3,2)\) gives \(4 - 6 + 2 = 0\text{,}\) while \(\vec{w} = (1,1,1)\) gives \(2 - 2 + 1 = 1 \neq 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.4.2 A Harder Example

    Example 2.33. When Does a Third Vector Fail to Enlarge the Span?

    Let
    \begin{equation*} \vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \vec{v}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ h \\ -4 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    where \(h\) is a real number.
    🔗
    1. For which values of \(h\) is \(\operatorname{Span}\{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, \vec{v}_3\} = \R^3\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. For the exceptional value of \(h\text{,}\) describe the span by an equation and write \(\vec{v}_3\) explicitly in terms of \(\vec{v}_1\) and \(\vec{v}_2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (1) The three vectors span \(\R^3\) exactly when \(A\vec{x} = \vec{b}\) is consistent for every \(\vec{b}\text{,}\) where \(A = \begin{bmatrix} \vec{v}_1 \amp \vec{v}_2 \amp \vec{v}_3 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) By Theorem 4.57, for a square matrix that happens exactly when \(A\) is invertible, i.e., when \(\det A \neq 0\text{.}\) Expanding along the first row,
    \begin{align*} \det A \amp = \begin{vmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp 1 \amp h \\ -1 \amp 1 \amp -4 \end{vmatrix}\\ \amp = 1(-4 - h) - 2(-8 + h) + 1(2 + 1)\\ \amp = -4 - h + 16 - 2h + 3 = 15 - 3h. \end{align*}
    So \(\det A = 0\) precisely when \(h = 5\text{,}\) and therefore
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Span}\{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, \vec{v}_3\} = \R^3 \qquad \text{for every } h \neq 5. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (2) Take \(h = 5\text{,}\) so \(\vec{v}_3 = (1,5,-4)\text{.}\) Solving \(a\vec{v}_1 + b\vec{v}_2 = \vec{v}_3\) gives \(a + 2b = 1\) and \(2a + b = 5\text{,}\) hence \(a = 3\) and \(b = -1\text{;}\) the third coordinate checks out, since \(-3 - 1 = -4\text{.}\) So
    \begin{equation*} \vec{v}_3 = 3\vec{v}_1 - \vec{v}_2, \end{equation*}
    which means \(\vec{v}_3\) adds nothing: \(\operatorname{Span}\{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, \vec{v}_3\} = \operatorname{Span}\{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2\}\text{,}\) a plane.
    🔗
    A vector perpendicular to both \(\vec{v}_1\) and \(\vec{v}_2\) is \(\vec{n} = (1,-1,-1)\text{,}\) since \(\vec{n} \cdot \vec{v}_1 = 1 - 2 + 1 = 0\) and \(\vec{n} \cdot \vec{v}_2 = 2 - 1 - 1 = 0\text{.}\) Hence, when \(h = 5\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Span}\{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, \vec{v}_3\} = \left\{ (x,y,z) \in \R^3 \; : \; x - y - z = 0 \right\}, \end{equation*}
    and indeed \(\vec{v}_3 = (1,5,-4)\) satisfies \(1 - 5 + 4 = 0\text{.}\)
