    Section 7.6 A Plane of Cats: Linear Combinations of Images

    The definition of a vector space says nothing about columns of numbers, so anything satisfying the axioms of Definition 7.1 may be added and scaled exactly as vectors in \(\R^n\) are. We have already seen in Example 7.3 that matrices form such a space. In this section we take the two pictures below — a cat alive in its box, and the same cat dead in the same box — read each one as a single vector, and form linear combinations of them.
     1 
    The two illustrations show Schrödinger’s cat, a famous thought experiment created by physicist Erwin Schrödinger in 1935. The physics is deferred to An Optional Connection to Quantum Mechanics at the end of the section; until then the pictures are simply two matrices.
    🔗
    Nothing here is new mathematics. Every step is scalar multiplication, addition, span, basis, and coordinates, applied in a vector space whose vectors happen to be pictures. That is the point of generalizing: the machinery built for arrows in the plane runs unchanged on a space of dimension four hundred thousand.

    Subsection 7.6.1 A Picture Is a Vector

    Let \(\mathbb{M}_{mn}\) denote the set of all \(m \times n\) matrices with real entries. The verification in Example 7.3 used nothing about the size \(2 \times 2\text{,}\) so it applies word for word to any fixed \(m\) and \(n\text{:}\) \(\mathbb{M}_{mn}\) is a vector space. Its dimension is \(mn\text{,}\) since the matrices \(E_{ij}\) with a \(1\) in position \((i,j)\) and zeros elsewhere span \(\mathbb{M}_{mn}\) and are linearly independent: a linear combination \(\sum_{i,j} c_{ij} E_{ij}\) is the matrix whose \((i,j)\) entry is \(c_{ij}\text{,}\) and that matrix is zero exactly when every \(c_{ij}\) is zero.
    🔗
    A grayscale picture is an element of this space. As in Digital Images as Matrices, a picture \(m\) pixels tall and \(n\) pixels wide is the \(m \times n\) matrix whose \((i,j)\) entry is the brightness of the pixel in row \(i\) and column \(j\text{,}\) on a scale where \(0\) is black and \(255\) is white. The two pictures in 7.33 are \(780\) pixels tall and \(520\) pixels wide, so each is a vector in \(\mathbb{M}_{780,520}\text{,}\) a space of dimension \(405{,}600\text{.}\) Call them
    \begin{equation*} A = \text{the alive cat}, \qquad D = \text{the dead cat}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    These two vectors are linearly independent. If one were a scalar multiple of the other, say \(A = cD\text{,}\) then the same \(c\) would have to work at every pixel. The pixel in row \(337\) and column \(256\text{,}\) in the cat’s left eye, has \(A\) entry \(210\) and \(D\) entry \(75\text{,}\) forcing \(c = \frac{210}{75} = \frac{14}{5}\text{;}\) the pixel in row \(601\) and column \(301\text{,}\) on the floor of the box, has \(A\) entry \(68\) and the same \(D\) entry \(75\text{,}\) forcing \(c = \frac{68}{75}\text{.}\) No single scalar does both, and reversing the roles of \(A\) and \(D\) fails for the same reason. So \(\{A, D\}\) is a linearly independent set of two vectors in a space of dimension \(405{,}600\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.6.2 Blending the Two Images

    The simplest linear combinations of \(A\) and \(D\) are the ones a screen can show. For a blend parameter \(\lambda\) between \(0\) and \(1\text{,}\) define
    \begin{equation*} S_{\lambda} = (1 - \lambda) A + \lambda D, \qquad 0 \leq \lambda \leq 1. \end{equation*}
    The coefficient of \(A\) is always \(1 - \lambda\) and the coefficient of \(D\) is always \(\lambda\text{.}\) These two numbers are nonnegative and add to \(1\text{,}\) so each entry of \(S_\lambda\) is a weighted average of the corresponding entries of \(A\) and \(D\) and therefore lies between them. Both of those lie between \(0\) and \(255\text{,}\) so every blend can be displayed exactly as it is computed. No rescaling and no normalization are needed anywhere in this subsection.
    🔗
    One parameter with one meaning runs through the whole row: \(\lambda\) is how far the blend has travelled from \(A\) towards \(D\text{.}\) The number that appears in the formula, in the caption, and in the pixel arithmetic of the next subsection is the same number every time.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.6.3 The Arithmetic Behind a Blend

    The pictures are made of arithmetic, and the arithmetic is small enough to inspect. Take from each picture the patch around the alive cat’s left eye — the same corner of the picture magnified in Digital Images as Matrices — and average it in blocks down to eight rows and six columns. From the alive cat this gives \(A_0\text{,}\) and from the dead cat, whose box is empty in that corner, it gives \(D_0\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} A_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 65 \amp 63 \amp 80 \amp 84 \amp 81 \amp 79 \\ 73 \amp 86 \amp 90 \amp 88 \amp 90 \amp 87 \\ 81 \amp 89 \amp 86 \amp 90 \amp 93 \amp 86 \\ 89 \amp 68 \amp 76 \amp 69 \amp 95 \amp 91 \\ 103 \amp 91 \amp 78 \amp 87 \amp 91 \amp 91 \\ 100 \amp 99 \amp 108 \amp 83 \amp 99 \amp 94 \\ 90 \amp 97 \amp 104 \amp 100 \amp 102 \amp 86 \\ 84 \amp 94 \amp 110 \amp 107 \amp 70 \amp 63 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad D_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 71 \amp 69 \amp 69 \amp 65 \amp 64 \amp 62 \\ 74 \amp 72 \amp 71 \amp 67 \amp 66 \amp 65 \\ 77 \amp 74 \amp 73 \amp 70 \amp 68 \amp 66 \\ 78 \amp 76 \amp 73 \amp 71 \amp 69 \amp 68 \\ 82 \amp 78 \amp 76 \amp 73 \amp 70 \amp 69 \\ 82 \amp 80 \amp 77 \amp 75 \amp 72 \amp 71 \\ 85 \amp 82 \amp 79 \amp 76 \amp 74 \amp 72 \\ 86 \amp 82 \amp 79 \amp 77 \amp 75 \amp 72 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The eye leaves \(A_0\) mottled: the pupil darkens the fourth row to \(68\) and \(69\text{,}\) and the bright iris below it pushes several entries past \(100\text{.}\) The empty wall leaves \(D_0\) a smooth gradient, every row a little lighter than the one above it and every column a little darker than the one to its left.
    🔗
    At \(\lambda = \frac{1}{2}\) both coefficients are \(\frac{1}{2}\text{,}\) so the blend is the average
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{2} A_0 + \frac{1}{2} D_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 68 \amp 66 \amp 74 \amp 74 \amp 72 \amp 70 \\ 74 \amp 79 \amp 80 \amp 78 \amp 78 \amp 76 \\ 79 \amp 82 \amp 80 \amp 80 \amp 80 \amp 76 \\ 84 \amp 72 \amp 74 \amp 70 \amp 82 \amp 80 \\ 92 \amp 84 \amp 77 \amp 80 \amp 80 \amp 80 \\ 91 \amp 90 \amp 92 \amp 79 \amp 86 \amp 82 \\ 88 \amp 90 \amp 92 \amp 88 \amp 88 \amp 79 \\ 85 \amp 88 \amp 94 \amp 92 \amp 72 \amp 68 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    rounded to integers. Every entry sits halfway between the two originals, which is why the eye in 7.36 survives the blend at half strength. In \(A_0\) the pupil entry \(68\) lies \(21\) below the \(89\) directly above it; in the average, \(72\) lies \(10\) below \(82\text{.}\) The contrast, like everything else, has been halved.

    Subsection 7.6.4 From Blends to Span

    That experiment separates three sets of pictures, each larger than the last. The blends themselves are the line segment
    \begin{equation*} L = \left\{ (1 - \lambda) A + \lambda D \; \middle| \; 0 \leq \lambda \leq 1 \right\}, \end{equation*}
    whose endpoints are \(A\) and \(D\text{.}\) Letting \(\lambda\) be any real number gives the whole line through \(A\) and \(D\text{,}\) the combinations \(xA + yD\) whose coefficients still satisfy \(x + y = 1\text{.}\) Dropping that condition too, and letting the two coefficients vary independently, gives everything: by Theorem 7.8,
    \begin{equation*} W = \operatorname{Span}\{A, D\} = \left\{ xA + yD \; \middle| \; x, y \in \R \right\} \end{equation*}
    is a subspace of \(\mathbb{M}_{780,520}\text{,}\) and since \(\{A, D\}\) is linearly independent it is a basis for \(W\text{.}\) So \(W\) is a two-dimensional subspace of a space of dimension \(405{,}600\text{:}\) a plane of cats, sitting inside an enormous space of pictures. The five pictures we displayed occupy a segment of one line in that plane.
    🔗
    Two dimensions means two coordinates. With \(\mathcal{B} = \{A, D\}\text{,}\) the coordinate vector of \(X = xA + yD\) in the sense of Definition 7.18 is
    \begin{equation*} [X]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} \in \R^2, \end{equation*}
    and by Theorem 7.17 no other pair of scalars produces the same picture. The coordinate mapping is an isomorphism from \(W\) to \(\R^2\text{,}\) so by Remark 7.14 the plane of cats is \(\R^2\) for every purpose that involves adding and scaling. Carrying the three sets of pictures across that isomorphism turns each one into a familiar subset of \(\R^2\text{,}\) as in 7.37: the blends become the segment joining \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\) to \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) the combinations with \(x + y = 1\) become the whole line through those two points, and \(W\) becomes all of \(\R^2\text{.}\) Everything on the right-hand side of that correspondence is a picture you could have drawn in a first course; the isomorphism is what lets us reason about a space of dimension \(405{,}600\) by drawing in the plane.

    Question 7.38. Do Displayable Pictures Form a Subspace?

    Only some of the vectors in \(W\) can be put on a screen, and it is worth saying what that means in the language of this chapter. Consider the set
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{P} = \left\{ M \in \mathbb{M}_{mn} \; \middle| \; 0 \leq m_{ij} \leq 255 \text{ for all } i, j \right\} \end{equation*}
    of pictures a screen can show. Is \(\mathcal{P}\) a subspace of \(\mathbb{M}_{mn}\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    No. \(\mathcal{P}\) contains \(A\) but not \(2A\text{,}\) so it fails closure under scalar multiplication, and it contains \(A\) and \(D\) but not \(A + D\text{:}\) the brightest entries of the two pictures are \(214\) and \(184\text{,}\) and their sum \(398\) is far outside the range. It is a bounded box inside the vector space, not a subspace of it. The vector space is the larger, simpler object: the arithmetic is unrestricted, and only the last step — looking at the answer — requires the entries to be in range. Blends are exactly the combinations for which that last step is free.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.6.5 Average and Difference

    A plane has more than one basis, and a second one is worth having here because its vectors mean something about images. Set
    \begin{equation*} M = \frac{1}{2}\left( A + D \right), \qquad \Delta = \frac{1}{2}\left( A - D \right). \end{equation*}
    The average \(M\) is the middle picture of 7.35, the blend at \(\lambda = \frac{1}{2}\text{.}\) The difference \(\Delta\) records where the two pictures disagree: its entries are close to zero on the box, the label, and the lighting, which the two pictures very nearly share, and they are largest where a cat is. Its entries run from \(-52\) to \(76\text{,}\) so it is not itself displayable: it is negative wherever the dead picture is the brighter of the two. For that reason 7.39 shows \(128J + \Delta\text{,}\) where \(J\) is the matrix of all ones; mid-gray then marks the pixels where the two pictures agree.
    🔗
    Adding and subtracting the two definitions recovers the original pictures,
    \begin{equation*} A = M + \Delta, \qquad D = M - \Delta, \end{equation*}
    so each of \(\{A, D\}\) and \(\{M, \Delta\}\) lies in the span of the other. Hence \(\mathcal{C} = \{M, \Delta\}\) is a second basis for the same plane \(W\text{.}\) Substituting these expressions into a blend gives
    \begin{equation*} S_{\lambda} = (1 - \lambda)(M + \Delta) + \lambda(M - \Delta) = M + (1 - 2\lambda) \Delta. \end{equation*}
    Read in this basis, the row of pictures in 7.35 does one thing only: the average component stays fixed at \(M\) while the difference coefficient \(1 - 2\lambda\) slides from \(+1\) down to \(-1\text{.}\) At \(\lambda = \frac{1}{2}\) the difference coefficient is \(0\text{,}\) and the blend is exactly the average.
    🔗
    The same substitution works for any vector of \(W\text{,}\) not just a blend. For \(X = xA + yD\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} X = x(M + \Delta) + y(M - \Delta) = (x + y) M + (x - y) \Delta, \end{equation*}
    which is the change of basis of Definition 7.23 made explicit:
    \begin{equation*} [X]_{\mathcal{C}} = P [X]_{\mathcal{B}}, \qquad P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    This \(P\) satisfies \(P^2 = 2 I_2\text{,}\) so the return trip is \(P^{-1} = \frac{1}{2} P\text{:}\) halving the sum and difference of the \(\mathcal{C}\)-coordinates recovers \(x\) and \(y\text{.}\) 7.41 puts the two descriptions of the same picture side by side.
    🔗
    The picture \(X\) has not changed; only its coordinates have, because the basis has. The alive cat is a basis vector of \(\mathcal{B}\) and has coordinates \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\) there, while in \(\mathcal{C}\) its coordinates are \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{:}\) a picture that is one basis vector in one basis is an even combination of both in the other. Being a combination of two things is not a property of the vector. It is a property of the vector together with the basis you chose to describe it, exactly as a vector in \(\R^2\) has no coordinates until a basis is named.

    Subsection 7.6.6 An Optional Connection to Quantum Mechanics

    Everything above is finished linear algebra, and none of what follows is needed for any of it. But the pictures were chosen for a reason, and the word for a linear combination of states in physics is worth meeting once.
    🔗

    Remark 7.42. For the Interested Reader.

    Quantum mechanics describes a system that can be found in either of two states by a linear combination of those states, called a superposition. Writing the two states as our two matrices, it would take the form \(\alpha A + \beta D\text{.}\) The resemblance to \(S_{\lambda}\) is real but limited. There the coefficients are amplitudes, generally complex numbers subject to \(|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1\text{,}\) and when the two states form an orthonormal basis their squared magnitudes \(|\alpha|^2\) and \(|\beta|^2\) are the probabilities of the two outcomes of a measurement. The blends in this section are classical linear combinations of two images, not representations of quantum states.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 7.43. This Is Not What a Superposition Really Is.

    Any picture of a superposition is a depiction, and the depiction will not be accurate: superposition has no faithful classical picture. The blends above are worth drawing because they show linear combinations, not because they show physics. We refer the interested reader to the following lecture by Richard Feynman on quantum mechanical behavior.
    🔗
    🔗
    Three differences are worth stating plainly.
    🔗
    • The real state space is complex. Quantum states are vectors in a complex vector space, so the amplitudes are complex numbers. All ten axioms of Definition 7.1 hold with \(\mathbb{C}\) in place of \(\R\text{,}\) and everything in this chapter carries over unchanged. Keeping the coefficients real, as we did, keeps the pictures drawable; what it leaves out is the phase a complex amplitude carries, and the interference that comes with it.
      🔗
      🔗
    • A superposition is not a blurred picture. Opening the box produces one cat or the other, never the blend in the middle of 7.35. A faded picture depicts the coefficients, not what anyone would see.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Adding pictures is not adding states. Averaging two pictures is a statement about brightness, and nothing physical happens when it is done. The combination means what it means here only because we declared these two pictures to stand for the two states; what you are looking at is a picture of \(1 - \lambda\) and \(\lambda\text{,}\) not of light leaving a box.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    What survives the caveats is the part this chapter is about. Two objects that are not columns of numbers — two photographs, or two quantum states — can be added and scaled, and once that is granted, span, linear independence, basis, dimension, coordinates, and change of basis all apply without a word of modification. That is what the axioms of Definition 7.1 bought us.
