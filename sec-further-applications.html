    Section 1.6 Further Applications: Heat, Levers, Triangles, and Magic Squares

    Polynomial interpolation was our first example of a problem that becomes a system of linear equations once the right unknowns have been named. This section collects four more, from physics, geometry, and recreational mathematics. In each of them the real work is the modeling: deciding what the unknowns are and translating the problem into equations. After that, the row reduction algorithm of Section 1.4 takes over.
    🔗
    Each example also carries a lesson about reading the answer. Row reduction describes every solution of the linear system, and it knows nothing else about the problem. Whether a solution makes sense — a weight must be positive, the entries of a magic square must be different whole numbers — is a separate question, and it is ours to ask.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.6.1 Temperatures on a Plate

    A thin square metal plate is insulated on both faces, and each of its four edges is held at a constant temperature. After a while the temperature at every point of the plate stops changing, and we would like to know these equilibrium temperatures. A simple model replaces the plate by a grid of points and uses the following rule.
    🔗

    Principle 1.57 The Averaging Rule.

    At equilibrium, the temperature at each interior grid point is the average of the temperatures at its four nearest grid points: the ones directly above it, below it, to its left, and to its right.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.59 Four Temperatures on a Square Plate.

    The plate in Figure 1.58 carries a grid with four interior points, whose equilibrium temperatures \(x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4\) are unknown. The top edge is held at \(80^\circ\text{,}\) the right edge at \(60^\circ\text{,}\) the bottom edge at \(40^\circ\text{,}\) and the left edge at \(20^\circ\text{.}\) Use the averaging rule to find the four temperatures.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The upper left point has the top edge above it, the left edge to its left, and the interior points with temperatures \(x_2\) and \(x_3\) to its right and below it. The other three points are similar, and the averaging rule gives one equation for each interior point:
    \begin{align*} x_1 \amp = \tfrac{1}{4}(80 + 20 + x_2 + x_3), \amp x_2 \amp = \tfrac{1}{4}(80 + 60 + x_1 + x_4),\\ x_3 \amp = \tfrac{1}{4}(20 + 40 + x_1 + x_4), \amp x_4 \amp = \tfrac{1}{4}(60 + 40 + x_2 + x_3). \end{align*}
    Multiplying each equation by \(4\) and collecting the unknowns on the left puts the system in standard form:
    \begin{align*} 4x_1 - x_2 - x_3 \phantom{{}- x_4} \amp = 100\\ -x_1 + 4x_2 \phantom{{}- x_3} - x_4 \amp = 140\\ -x_1 \phantom{{}+ 4x_2} + 4x_3 - x_4 \amp = 60\\ \phantom{-x_1} - x_2 - x_3 + 4x_4 \amp = 100 \end{align*}
    🔗
    Now row reduce. A little planning keeps fractions out of the forward phase. (When two operations are attached to one arrow, the upper one is performed first.)
    \begin{align*} \left[ \begin{array}{cccc|c} 4 \amp -1 \amp -1 \amp 0 \amp 100 \\ -1 \amp 4 \amp 0 \amp -1 \amp 140 \\ -1 \amp 0 \amp 4 \amp -1 \amp 60 \\ 0 \amp -1 \amp -1 \amp 4 \amp 100 \end{array} \right] \amp \xrightarrow[-r_1]{r_1 \leftrightarrow r_2} \left[ \begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 \amp -4 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp -140 \\ 4 \amp -1 \amp -1 \amp 0 \amp 100 \\ -1 \amp 0 \amp 4 \amp -1 \amp 60 \\ 0 \amp -1 \amp -1 \amp 4 \amp 100 \end{array} \right]\\ \amp \xrightarrow[r_1 + r_3]{-4r_1 + r_2} \left[ \begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 \amp -4 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp -140 \\ 0 \amp 15 \amp -1 \amp -4 \amp 660 \\ 0 \amp -4 \amp 4 \amp 0 \amp -80 \\ 0 \amp -1 \amp -1 \amp 4 \amp 100 \end{array} \right]\\ \amp \xrightarrow[r_2 \leftrightarrow r_3]{-\frac{1}{4} r_3} \left[ \begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 \amp -4 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp -140 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -1 \amp 0 \amp 20 \\ 0 \amp 15 \amp -1 \amp -4 \amp 660 \\ 0 \amp -1 \amp -1 \amp 4 \amp 100 \end{array} \right]\\ \amp \xrightarrow[r_2 + r_4]{-15r_2 + r_3} \left[ \begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 \amp -4 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp -140 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -1 \amp 0 \amp 20 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 14 \amp -4 \amp 360 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp -2 \amp 4 \amp 120 \end{array} \right]\\ \amp \xrightarrow[r_3 \leftrightarrow r_4]{-\frac{1}{2} r_4} \left[ \begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 \amp -4 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp -140 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -1 \amp 0 \amp 20 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp -2 \amp -60 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 14 \amp -4 \amp 360 \end{array} \right]\\ \amp \xrightarrow{-14r_3 + r_4} \left[ \begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 \amp -4 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp -140 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -1 \amp 0 \amp 20 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp -2 \amp -60 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 24 \amp 1200 \end{array} \right]. \end{align*}
    There are four pivots and no free variables, so the solution is unique. Back substitution gives
    \begin{align*} x_4 \amp = \tfrac{1200}{24} = 50, \amp x_3 \amp = -60 + 2x_4 = 40,\\ x_2 \amp = 20 + x_3 = 60, \amp x_1 \amp = -140 + 4x_2 - x_4 = 50. \end{align*}
    🔗
    The equilibrium temperatures are \(50^\circ\text{,}\) \(60^\circ\text{,}\) \(40^\circ\text{,}\) and \(50^\circ\text{.}\) As a check, the neighbors of the upper right point average to \(\tfrac{1}{4}(80 + 60 + 50 + 50) = 60\text{.}\) The answer is also plausible. All four values lie between the coldest edge and the warmest, as they must, since an average never falls outside the range of the numbers averaged. The warmest interior point, \(x_2\text{,}\) is the one next to the two warmest edges, and the coolest, \(x_3\text{,}\) is next to the two coolest.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Four grid points give only a rough picture of the plate. A finer grid gives a better one, at a price: with \(100\) interior points in each direction there are \(10{,}000\) unknowns and \(10{,}000\) equations. Nobody solves such a system by hand, but the algorithm is the same, and this is the kind of problem for which it was designed. Notice also that every equation, however fine the grid, involves at most five of the unknowns.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.6.2 Balancing Levers

    A lever is a rigid bar that is free to turn about one of its points, the pivot. Archimedes found the rule that decides when it stays level.
    🔗

    Principle 1.60 The Law of the Lever.

    Two weights hanging from a lever, on opposite sides of the pivot, balance exactly when the products
    \begin{equation*} (\text{weight}) \times (\text{distance from the pivot}) \end{equation*}
    are equal on the two sides.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.62 A Mobile That Balances.

    The mobile in Figure 1.61 consists of three levers. The main bar hangs from the ceiling, and two smaller bars hang from its ends, at distances \(4\) and \(3\) from its pivot. The left bar carries weights \(w_1\) and \(w_2\) at distances \(1\) and \(2\) from its pivot, and the right bar carries weights \(w_3\) and \(w_4\) at distances \(1\) and \(3\) from its pivot. The bars and strings are light enough to ignore.
    1. Find all weights \(w_1, w_2, w_3, w_4\) for which all three levers balance.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the weights if the whole mobile is to weigh \(14\) ounces.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Can the mobile balance with four equal weights?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) The law of the lever gives one equation for each bar. For the left bar, \(1 \cdot w_1 = 2 \cdot w_2\text{,}\) and for the right bar, \(1 \cdot w_3 = 3 \cdot w_4\text{.}\) Each end of the main bar carries the total weight hanging below it, so the main bar balances when \(4(w_1 + w_2) = 3(w_3 + w_4)\text{.}\) In standard form,
    \begin{align*} w_1 - 2w_2 \phantom{{}- 3w_3 - 3w_4} \amp = 0\\ w_3 - 3w_4 \amp = 0\\ 4w_1 + 4w_2 - 3w_3 - 3w_4 \amp = 0 \end{align*}
    and row reduction gives
    \begin{align*} \left[ \begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 \amp -2 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp -3 \amp 0 \\ 4 \amp 4 \amp -3 \amp -3 \amp 0 \end{array} \right] \amp \xrightarrow[r_2 \leftrightarrow r_3]{-4r_1 + r_3} \left[ \begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 \amp -2 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 12 \amp -3 \amp -3 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp -3 \amp 0 \end{array} \right]\\ \amp \xrightarrow[\frac{1}{12} r_2]{3r_3 + r_2} \left[ \begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 \amp -2 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \amp -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp -3 \amp 0 \end{array} \right] \xrightarrow{2r_2 + r_1} \left[ \begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp -2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \amp -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp -3 \amp 0 \end{array} \right]. \end{align*}
    The variable \(w_4\) is free. Setting \(w_4 = t\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} w_1 = 2t, \qquad w_2 = t, \qquad w_3 = 3t, \qquad w_4 = t. \end{equation*}
    The linear system has a solution for every real \(t\text{,}\) but a weight must be positive, so the mobile balances exactly when \(t > 0\text{.}\) We could have predicted infinitely many solutions: there are more unknowns than equations, and the system is consistent because the empty mobile, with every weight zero, is a solution. It also makes physical sense. Doubling every weight doubles both sides of each balance equation, so only the proportions \(2 : 1 : 3 : 1\) matter.
    🔗
    (b) The total weight is \(2t + t + 3t + t = 7t\text{,}\) so \(7t = 14\) and \(t = 2\text{:}\) the weights are \(4\text{,}\) \(2\text{,}\) \(6\text{,}\) and \(2\) ounces. In terms of the system, we have added a fourth equation, \(w_1 + w_2 + w_3 + w_4 = 14\text{,}\) and the solution is now unique.
    🔗
    (c) No. Equal weights would require \(w_1 = w_2\text{,}\) that is, \(2t = t\text{,}\) which forces \(t = 0\text{.}\) With these distances, the only way to have four equal weights is to have no weights at all.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.6.3 The Law of Cosines

    In a triangle with sides \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) \(c\text{,}\) let \(\alpha\text{,}\) \(\beta\text{,}\) \(\gamma\) be the angles opposite those sides, as in Figure 1.63. The perpendicular from the vertex \(A\) meets the opposite side at a point \(D\) and cuts that side into two pieces. The two right triangles in the figure show that the pieces have lengths \(c\cos\beta\) and \(b\cos\gamma\text{,}\) so \(a = b\cos\gamma + c\cos\beta\text{.}\) Dropping perpendiculars from the other two vertices gives two more equations of the same kind:
    \begin{align*} c\cos\beta + b\cos\gamma \amp = a\\ c\cos\alpha + a\cos\gamma \amp = b\\ b\cos\alpha + a\cos\beta \amp = c \end{align*}
    (If one of the angles is larger than \(90^\circ\text{,}\) a perpendicular lands outside the triangle and one cosine is negative, but the three equations remain true.) Regard the sides as known. Then these are three linear equations in the three unknowns \(\cos\alpha\text{,}\) \(\cos\beta\text{,}\) \(\cos\gamma\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 1.64 Solving for the Cosines.

    Solve the three equations above for \(\cos\alpha\text{,}\) \(\cos\beta\text{,}\) and \(\cos\gamma\) in terms of the sides \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) \(c\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    With the unknowns in the order \(\cos\alpha\text{,}\) \(\cos\beta\text{,}\) \(\cos\gamma\text{,}\) the augmented matrix is \(\left[ \begin{array}{ccc|c} 0 \amp c \amp b \amp a \\ c \amp 0 \amp a \amp b \\ b \amp a \amp 0 \amp c \end{array} \right]\text{.}\) Its entries are letters, and row reducing it means dividing by them. (That is legitimate here, since side lengths are positive.) A change of unknowns makes the arithmetic much friendlier. Multiply the three equations by \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) and \(c\text{,}\) respectively, and put
    \begin{equation*} u = bc\cos\alpha, \qquad v = ca\cos\beta, \qquad w = ab\cos\gamma. \end{equation*}
    The system becomes
    \begin{align*} v + w \amp = a^2\\ u \phantom{{}+ v} + w \amp = b^2\\ u + v \phantom{{}+ w} \amp = c^2 \end{align*}
    in which every coefficient is \(0\) or \(1\text{,}\) and
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|c} 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \amp a^2 \\ 1 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp b^2 \\ 1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \amp c^2 \end{array} \right] \xrightarrow[-r_1 + r_3]{r_1 \leftrightarrow r_2} \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp b^2 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \amp a^2 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -1 \amp c^2 - b^2 \end{array} \right] \xrightarrow{-r_2 + r_3} \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp b^2 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \amp a^2 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp -2 \amp c^2 - b^2 - a^2 \end{array} \right]. \end{equation*}
    There are three pivots whatever the values of \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) \(c\text{,}\) so the solution is unique, and no cases need to be separated. Back substitution gives
    \begin{equation*} w = \frac{a^2 + b^2 - c^2}{2}, \qquad v = a^2 - w = \frac{a^2 + c^2 - b^2}{2}, \qquad u = b^2 - w = \frac{b^2 + c^2 - a^2}{2}, \end{equation*}
    and dividing by \(bc\text{,}\) \(ca\text{,}\) and \(ab\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \cos\alpha = \frac{b^2 + c^2 - a^2}{2bc}, \qquad \cos\beta = \frac{a^2 + c^2 - b^2}{2ac}, \qquad \cos\gamma = \frac{a^2 + b^2 - c^2}{2ab}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The first formula, rearranged, is the law of cosines: \(a^2 = b^2 + c^2 - 2bc\cos\alpha\text{.}\) When \(\alpha = 90^\circ\) the last term vanishes and it becomes the Pythagorean theorem.
    🔗
    As a check, take the triangle of Figure 1.63, with \(a = 14\text{,}\) \(b = 15\text{,}\) \(c = 13\text{.}\) Then \(u = 99\text{,}\) \(v = 70\text{,}\) \(w = 126\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} \cos\alpha = \frac{99}{15 \cdot 13} = \frac{33}{65}, \qquad \cos\beta = \frac{70}{14 \cdot 13} = \frac{5}{13}, \qquad \cos\gamma = \frac{126}{14 \cdot 15} = \frac{3}{5}. \end{equation*}
    The figure agrees: the pieces of the base are \(c\cos\beta = 13 \cdot \tfrac{5}{13} = 5\) and \(b\cos\gamma = 15 \cdot \tfrac{3}{5} = 9\text{,}\) and \(5 + 9 = 14\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    A system whose coefficients contain parameters usually forces us to separate cases, as in Example 1.51. Here a well-chosen change of unknowns removed the parameters from the coefficients altogether. Choosing the unknowns is part of the modeling, and it is worth a moment’s thought.

    Subsection 1.6.4 Magic Squares

    A magic square of size \(n\) is an \(n \times n\) array that contains each of the whole numbers \(1, 2, \ldots, n^2\) exactly once, and in which every row, every column, and both diagonals add up to the same number. That common sum is forced. The entries add up to \(1 + 2 + \cdots + n^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}n^2(n^2+1)\text{,}\) and this total is shared equally among the \(n\) rows, so each row adds up to \(\tfrac{1}{2}n(n^2+1)\text{.}\) For \(n = 3\) the magic sum is \(15\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The conditions on the sums are linear equations in the entries, so a linear system can do most of the work. The condition that the entries be the different numbers \(1, 2, \ldots, n^2\) is not a linear equation, and it has to be checked separately.
    🔗

    Example 1.65 No Magic Square of Size 2.

    Suppose that both rows, both columns, and both diagonals of \(\begin{bmatrix} a \amp b \\ c \amp d \end{bmatrix}\) add up to the same number \(s\text{.}\) Show that \(a = b = c = d\text{,}\) and conclude that there is no magic square of size \(2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The six conditions are six linear equations in the four unknowns \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) \(c\text{,}\) \(d\text{,}\) with \(s\) treated as a constant:
    \begin{align*} E_1\colon\; a + b \amp = s, \amp E_3\colon\; a + c \amp = s, \amp E_5\colon\; a + d \amp = s,\\ E_2\colon\; c + d \amp = s, \amp E_4\colon\; b + d \amp = s, \amp E_6\colon\; b + c \amp = s. \end{align*}
    The replacement \(-E_1 + E_3\) turns the third equation into \(c - b = 0\text{,}\) and \(-E_3 + E_5\text{,}\) applied to the original equations, gives \(d - c = 0\text{.}\) So \(b = c = d\text{.}\) Then \(E_6\) reads \(2b = s\text{,}\) so \(b = \tfrac{s}{2}\text{,}\) and \(E_1\) gives \(a = s - b = \tfrac{s}{2}\) as well. Conversely, \(a = b = c = d = \tfrac{s}{2}\) satisfies all six equations.
    🔗
    So this system, with more equations than unknowns, is consistent and has exactly one solution, in which all four entries are equal. A magic square of size \(2\) would need the four different entries \(1, 2, 3, 4\text{,}\) with \(s = 5\text{;}\) the only solution of the equations has every entry equal to \(\tfrac{5}{2}\text{.}\) The linear system has a solution, but the problem does not.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.66 Completing a Magic Square of Size 3.

    Find all magic squares of size \(3\) whose top row is \(4, 9, 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Name the six unknown entries:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 9 \amp 2 \\ a \amp b \amp c \\ d \amp e \amp f \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The top row already adds up to \(15\text{.}\) The other two rows, the three columns, and the two diagonals give seven equations in the six unknowns:
    \begin{align*} a + b + c \amp = 15, \amp d + e + f \amp = 15, \amp \amp\\ a + d \amp = 11, \amp b + e \amp = 6, \amp c + f \amp = 13,\\ b + f \amp = 11, \amp b + d \amp = 13. \amp \amp \end{align*}
    (For instance, the first column gives \(4 + a + d = 15\text{,}\) and the diagonal from the upper left gives \(4 + b + f = 15\text{.}\)) More equations than unknowns does not by itself mean that there are no solutions, and it is worth looking at the equations before reducing a \(7 \times 7\) augmented matrix. Three of them express \(e\text{,}\) \(f\text{,}\) and \(d\) through \(b\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} e = 6 - b, \qquad f = 11 - b, \qquad d = 13 - b. \end{equation*}
    Substituting into \(d + e + f = 15\) gives \(30 - 3b = 15\text{,}\) so \(b = 5\text{.}\) Then \(d = 8\text{,}\) \(e = 1\text{,}\) \(f = 6\text{,}\) and the first and third columns give \(a = 11 - d = 3\) and \(c = 13 - f = 7\text{.}\)
    🔗
    One equation, \(a + b + c = 15\text{,}\) was never used. It holds, since \(3 + 5 + 7 = 15\text{,}\) so the system is consistent, with the unique solution
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 9 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp 5 \amp 7 \\ 8 \amp 1 \amp 6 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The seventh equation was redundant: the reduced row-echelon form of the augmented matrix, computed in the Sage cell below, has six pivots and one zero row.
    🔗
    It remains to check the condition that the equations could not see. The entries are the numbers \(1\) through \(9\text{,}\) each used once. So there is exactly one magic square with this top row.
