    1. Which Combinations Are Defined?

    Let
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 3 \\ 2 \amp 1 \amp 7 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp -1 \amp 2 \\ -3 \amp 2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad E = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Find, if possible, \(-3A\text{,}\) \(3B - A\text{,}\) \(AC\text{,}\) \(CB\text{,}\) \(AE\text{,}\) and \(EA\text{.}\) If a combination is not possible, explain why.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Scalar multiple and sum. Both are computed entry by entry, and \(A\) and \(B\) have the same size, so both are defined:
    \begin{equation*} -3A = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \amp -6 \amp -9 \\ -6 \amp -3 \amp -21 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad 3B - A = \begin{bmatrix} 8 \amp -5 \amp 3 \\ -11 \amp 5 \amp -4 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \(AC\) is not possible. \(A\) is \(2 \times 3\) and \(C\) is \(2 \times 2\text{.}\) By Remark 4.14 the product needs the number of columns of \(A\) to equal the number of rows of \(C\text{,}\) and \(3 \neq 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(CB\) is defined and is \(2 \times 3\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} CB = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp -1 \amp 2 \\ -3 \amp 2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \amp 3 \amp 4 \\ 6 \amp -1 \amp 7 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \(AE\) and \(EA\) are not possible. \(A\) is \(2 \times 3\) and \(E\) is \(2 \times 1\text{:}\) for \(AE\) we would need \(3 = 2\text{,}\) and for \(EA\) we would need \(1 = 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. ★ Reading Entries off a Matrix.

    Let
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp -1 \amp 2 \\ 0 \amp 4 \amp 1 \\ -2 \amp 0 \amp 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a_{ij} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Give the size of \(A\text{,}\) the entries \(a_{23}\) and \(a_{31}\text{,}\) and the main diagonal.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(A\) has three rows and three columns, so it is a \(3 \times 3\) matrix.
    🔗
    The entry \(a_{23}\) sits in row \(2\text{,}\) column \(3\text{,}\) so \(a_{23} = 1\text{.}\) The entry \(a_{31}\) sits in row \(3\text{,}\) column \(1\text{,}\) so \(a_{31} = -2\text{.}\) Row first, then column — the two are different entries here, which is a useful check on the convention.
    🔗
    The main diagonal runs from the upper left to the lower right and consists of \(a_{11} = 3\text{,}\) \(a_{22} = 4\text{,}\) \(a_{33} = 5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. ★ Zero and Identity Matrices.

    Write down \(0_{3 \times 3}\) and \(I_2\text{,}\) and verify that \(A + 0_{2 \times 2} = A\) and \(I_2 A = A\) for \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \amp -2 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} 0_{3 \times 3} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad I_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Adding the zero matrix changes nothing, since addition is entry by entry:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 5 \amp -2 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \amp -2 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} = A. \checkmark \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Multiplying by the identity also changes nothing:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 5 \amp -2 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \amp -2 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} = A. \checkmark \end{equation*}
    The first row of \(I_2\) selects the first row of \(A\text{,}\) and the second selects the second.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. ★ Adding Two Matrices.

    Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(B = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 1 \\ -2 \amp 5 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Compute \(A + B\) and \(A - B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Addition and subtraction are entry by entry:
    \begin{equation*} A + B = \begin{bmatrix} 2 + 4 \amp -1 + 1 \\ 0 + (-2) \amp 3 + 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 \amp 0 \\ -2 \amp 8 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} A - B = \begin{bmatrix} 2 - 4 \amp -1 - 1 \\ 0 + 2 \amp 3 - 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \amp -2 \\ 2 \amp -2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For instance the \((1,2)\)-entry of the sum is \(a_{12} + b_{12} = -1 + 1 = 0\text{.}\) Both operations require the two matrices to have the same size.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. ★ Multiplying by a Scalar.

    If \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) compute \(-3A\) and \(\tfrac{1}{2}A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Every entry is multiplied by the scalar:
    \begin{equation*} -3A = \begin{bmatrix} -6 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp -9 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \tfrac{1}{2}A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -\frac{1}{2} \\[2pt] 0 \amp \frac{3}{2} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Note that the zero entry stays zero whatever the scalar.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6. ★ Combining Scalar Multiples.

    Simplify
    \begin{equation*} 4 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp -1 \\ -3 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} - 2 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp -1 \\ -3 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -6 \amp 3 \\ 9 \amp -3 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Write \(M = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp -1 \\ -3 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) The third matrix is also a multiple of \(M\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} -6 \amp 3 \\ 9 \amp -3 \end{bmatrix} = -3M. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    So by property (2) of Theorem 4.11, the whole expression collapses to a single scalar multiple:
    \begin{align*} 4M - 2M + (-3M) \amp = (4 - 2 - 3)M\\ \amp = -M\\ \amp = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \amp 1 \\ 3 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{align*}
    🔗

    7. Products in Both Orders.

    Let
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ -2 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \amp -2 \\ 2 \amp 1 \amp -2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \amp -3 \\ -1 \amp 2 \amp 0 \\ -3 \amp -1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Find, if possible, \(AB\text{,}\) \(BA\text{,}\) \(AC\text{,}\) \(CA\text{,}\) \(BC\text{,}\) and \(CB\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The sizes are \(A_{3 \times 2}\text{,}\) \(B_{2 \times 3}\text{,}\) \(C_{3 \times 3}\text{.}\) Checking inner dimensions:
    \begin{equation*} AB = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp 0 \amp -4 \\ -4 \amp 1 \amp 6 \\ 5 \amp 1 \amp -6 \end{bmatrix}_{3 \times 3}, \qquad BA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -2 \\ -2 \amp -3 \end{bmatrix}_{2 \times 2}, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} CA = \begin{bmatrix} -4 \amp -6 \\ -5 \amp -3 \\ -1 \amp -2 \end{bmatrix}_{3 \times 2}, \qquad BC = \begin{bmatrix} 8 \amp 1 \amp -3 \\ 7 \amp 6 \amp -6 \end{bmatrix}_{2 \times 3}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \(AC\) is not possible, since \(A\) has \(2\) columns while \(C\) has \(3\) rows; and \(CB\) is not possible, since \(C\) has \(3\) columns while \(B\) has \(2\) rows.
    🔗
    Note that \(AB\) and \(BA\) are both defined here but are not even the same size, a stronger failure of commutativity than Example 4.19.

    8. A Product of Three Matrices.

    Compute and simplify
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a \amp b \\ a \amp -b \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Matrix multiplication is associative, so group whichever way is easier. Taking the first two factors,
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a \amp b \\ a \amp -b \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2a \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    and then
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 2a \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2a \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The \(b\)s cancel because the two rows of the middle matrix agree in the first column and differ only in sign in the second.

    9. A Single Entry of a Product.

    Find the \((2,3)\)-entry of \(AB\text{,}\) where
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 4 \amp -2 \\ 1 \amp -2 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp 1 \amp -9 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 17 \amp 6 \amp 4 \\ 10 \amp -9 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp 0 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    There is no need to compute the whole product. By Theorem 4.16, the \((2,3)\)-entry is row \(2\) of \(A\) against column \(3\) of \(B\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} (AB)_{23} = 1 \cdot 4 + (-2) \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 3 = 4 - 4 + 3 = 3. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    10. Nilpotent Matrices.

    A square matrix \(A\) is called nilpotent if \(A^2 = 0\text{.}\) Which of
    \begin{equation*} B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad D = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    are nilpotent?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Square each one:
    \begin{equation*} B^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} C^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \neq 0, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} D^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    So \(B\) and \(D\) are nilpotent, and \(C\) is not.
    🔗
    Note that \(C\) instead satisfies \(C^2 = C\text{,}\) the condition studied in Exercise 10.4.11.

    11. Matrices with \(A^2 = A\).

    Let \(A\) be an \(n \times n\) matrix and \(I\) the \(n \times n\) identity. Which of the following statements is always true?
    1. If \(A^2 = A\text{,}\) then \(A = 0\) or \(A = I\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \(A^2 = A\) and \(A \neq 0\text{,}\) then \(A\) is invertible.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. If \(A^2 = A\text{,}\) then \(\det A = 0\) or \(\det A = 1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. If \(\det A = 0\) or \(\det A = 1\text{,}\) then \(A^2 = A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    A single example settles (a) and (b). Take \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Then \(A^2 = A\text{,}\) yet \(A\) is neither \(0\) nor \(I\text{,}\) and \(\det A = 0\) so \(A\) is not invertible. Both (a) and (b) are false.
    🔗
    (c) is true. Taking determinants in \(A^2 = A\) and using Theorem 6.19,
    \begin{equation*} (\det A)^2 = \det A, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \det A (\det A - 1) = 0, \end{equation*}
    which forces \(\det A = 0\) or \(\det A = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (d) is false — it is the converse of (c), and converses of true statements need not hold. For instance \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\) has \(\det A = 1\text{,}\) but \(A^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \neq A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    12. When Cancellation Fails.

    1. Find \(2 \times 2\) matrices \(A\text{,}\) \(C\text{,}\) \(D\) with \(A \neq 0\) and \(C \neq D\text{,}\) but \(AC = AD\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Can this be done with \(AC \neq 0\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Give an example of matrices \(A\text{,}\) \(B\text{,}\) neither of them zero, with \(AB = 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) The condition \(AC = AD\) is the same as \(A(C - D) = 0\text{,}\) so we need a nonzero \(A\) that kills some nonzero matrix. Any \(A\) whose columns are linearly dependent will do. Take
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \\ -1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad D = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 2 \\ 2 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Every column of \(C\) and of \(D\) has equal entries, and \(A\) annihilates such a column, so
    \begin{equation*} AC = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} = AD, \end{equation*}
    while \(C \neq D\) and \(A \neq 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) Yes. We only need \(A(C-D) = 0\text{;}\) nothing forces \(AC\) itself to vanish. Take
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad D = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Then
    \begin{equation*} AC = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} = AD \neq 0, \end{equation*}
    and still \(C \neq D\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (c) This is the case \(D = 0\) of part (a): we need a nonzero \(B\) whose columns all solve \(A\vec{x} = \vec{0}\text{.}\) Such a nonzero \(\vec{x}\) exists exactly when \(A\) is not invertible. With the same \(A\) as in (a),
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \\ -1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Note that \(B\) may have any number of columns; a single column \([1\ 1]^T\) works just as well.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    13. ★ Computing a Product Column by Column.

    Find \(AB\text{,}\) where
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp -1 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 3 \amp -2 \end{bmatrix}_{2 \times 3}, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp -1 \\ 2 \amp 1 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp -2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}_{3 \times 3}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(A\) is \(2 \times 3\) and \(B\) is \(3 \times 3\text{,}\) so \(AB\) is defined and is \(2 \times 3\text{.}\) Compute \(A\vec{B}_j\) for each column of \(B\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} A\vec{B}_1 \amp = A \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix},\\ A\vec{B}_2 \amp = A \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix},\\ A\vec{B}_3 \amp = A \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Assembling these as columns,
    \begin{equation*} AB = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp -1 \amp -5 \\ 7 \amp 7 \amp 6 \end{bmatrix}_{2 \times 3}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    14. ★ Computing a Single Entry.

    With \(A\) and \(B\) as in Exercise 10.4.13 and \(C = AB\text{,}\) compute \(c_{13}\) without computing all of \(C\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.

    17. ★ A Product of Commuting Matrices.

    Let \(A\text{,}\) \(B\text{,}\) \(C\) be \(n \times n\) matrices. Prove that if \(A\) and \(B\) each commute with \(C\text{,}\) then \(AB\) commutes with \(C\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Suppose \(AC = CA\) and \(BC = CB\text{.}\) Then, moving \(C\) leftwards past \(B\) and then past \(A\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} (AB)C \amp = A(BC) \amp\amp \text{(associativity)}\\ \amp = A(CB) \amp\amp (B \text{ commutes with } C)\\ \amp = (AC)B \amp\amp \text{(associativity)}\\ \amp = (CA)B \amp\amp (A \text{ commutes with } C)\\ \amp = C(AB) \amp\amp \text{(associativity)} \end{align*}
    using Theorem 4.20 at each step. Hence \(AB\) and \(C\) commute.
    🔗
    Associativity is doing all the work: at no point did we need to swap two matrices that were not assumed to commute.

    18. Sizes, Transposes, and Three False Claims.

    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 2 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Which of the following statements are incorrect?
    1. \(AB \neq BA\text{,}\) since they have different sizes.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(BA = I_2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(A = B^T\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(AB = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 2 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. \(BA\) is not invertible.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Compute both products. \(A\) is \(3 \times 2\) and \(B\) is \(2 \times 3\text{,}\) so \(AB\) is \(3 \times 3\) and \(BA\) is \(2 \times 2\text{:}\)

    🔗
    (a) Correct. The two products are \(3 \times 3\) and \(2 \times 2\text{,}\) so they cannot be equal.
    🔗
    (b) Incorrect. \(BA = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \neq I_2\text{.}\) The \((1,1)\)-entry is \(1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 2 = 5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (c) Correct. Transposing \(B\) turns its rows into columns, giving exactly \(A\text{;}\) see Definition 4.23.
    🔗
    (d) Incorrect. The third column of \(AB\) is \([2\ 0\ 4]^T\text{,}\) not \([0\ 0\ 0]^T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (e) Incorrect. \(BA\) is diagonal with nonzero diagonal entries, so it is invertible, with \((BA)^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/5 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    So three of the five statements fail. The exercise is a useful reminder that \(BA = I\) is a genuine condition to be checked, not something guaranteed by \(A = B^T\text{.}\)

    22. When Cancellation Works.

    Explain why, if \(AB = AC\) and \(A^{-1}\) exists, then \(B = C\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Multiply both sides on the left by \(A^{-1}\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} A^{-1}(AB) \amp = A^{-1}(AC)\\ (A^{-1}A)B \amp = (A^{-1}A)C\\ IB \amp = IC\\ B \amp = C. \end{align*}
    The second step is associativity of matrix multiplication (Theorem 4.20), and the last uses that \(I\) is the identity for multiplication.
    🔗
    Invertibility is essential, not decorative: Exercise 10.4.12 shows that cancellation can fail outright when \(A^{-1}\) does not exist. Note also that we must multiply on the same side on both sides of the equation — left-cancelling \(A\) tells us nothing about an equation of the form \(BA = CA\text{.}\)

    23. Inverses of Transposes and Products.

    1. Show that if \(A\) is an invertible \(n \times n\) matrix, then so is \(A^T\text{,}\) and \((A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Show that \((AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}\) by verifying both \(AB(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = I\) and \(B^{-1}A^{-1}(AB) = I\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Show that \((ABC)^{-1} = C^{-1}B^{-1}A^{-1}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Throughout you may use that an inverse, when it exists, is unique (Theorem 4.33): if \(MN = I\) and \(NM = I\text{,}\) then \(N = M^{-1}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) We show that \((A^{-1})^T\) behaves like an inverse of \(A^T\text{.}\) Using \(M^T N^T = (NM)^T\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} A^T (A^{-1})^T = (A^{-1}A)^T = I^T = I, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} (A^{-1})^T A^T = (AA^{-1})^T = I^T = I. \end{equation*}
    By uniqueness of the inverse, \(A^T\) is invertible with \((A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) Regroup and cancel from the inside out:
    \begin{equation*} (AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = AIA^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} (B^{-1}A^{-1})(AB) = B^{-1}(A^{-1}A)B = B^{-1}IB = B^{-1}B = I. \end{equation*}
    So \(B^{-1}A^{-1}\) acts as the inverse of \(AB\text{,}\) and by uniqueness \((AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (c) The same peeling, one layer deeper:
    \begin{align*} (ABC)(C^{-1}B^{-1}A^{-1}) \amp = AB(CC^{-1})B^{-1}A^{-1}\\ \amp = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I, \end{align*}
    \begin{align*} (C^{-1}B^{-1}A^{-1})(ABC) \amp = C^{-1}B^{-1}(A^{-1}A)BC\\ \amp = C^{-1}(B^{-1}B)C = C^{-1}C = I. \end{align*}
    Both (b) and (c) are instances of Theorem 4.47: inverting a product reverses the order of the factors, just as transposing one does.

      🔗

      🔗
    🔗

    30. Matrix Equations with Invertible Coefficients.

    1. Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 3 \\ 1 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 3 \\ 2 \amp 5 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) If \(AYB = C\text{,}\) find \(Y\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 3 \\ 1 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) What can be said about the solutions \(Y\) of \(AY = BA\text{,}\) where \(Y\) and \(B\) are \(2 \times 2\) matrices?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) Both \(A\) and \(B\) are invertible: \(\det A = 4 - 3 = 1\) and \(\det B = 5 - 6 = -1\text{.}\) Multiply the equation by \(A^{-1}\) on the left and \(B^{-1}\) on the right:
    \begin{equation*} Y = A^{-1} C B^{-1}. \end{equation*}
    Now
    \begin{equation*} A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp -3 \\ -1 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B^{-1} = -\begin{bmatrix} 5 \amp -3 \\ -2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -5 \amp 3 \\ 2 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}

    37. Writing a Matrix as a Product of Elementary Matrices.

    Express \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 5 \\ 1 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix}\) as a product of three elementary matrices.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Row reduce \(A\) to \(I\text{,}\) recording each operation:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 5 \\ 1 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{r_1 \leftrightarrow r_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp 5 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \xrightarrow{\frac{1}{5}r_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{-3r_2 + r_1} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The corresponding elementary matrices, each obtained by doing the operation to \(I\text{,}\) are
    \begin{equation*} E_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad E_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp \frac{1}{5} \end{bmatrix}, \quad E_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    and \(E_3 E_2 E_1 A = I\text{.}\) Solving for \(A\) and using Exercise 10.4.23,
    \begin{equation*} A = E_1^{-1} E_2^{-1} E_3^{-1}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Each inverse is elementary, being the matrix of the reversing operation (Example 4.54):
    \begin{equation*} E_1^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad E_2^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 5 \end{bmatrix}, \quad E_3^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    So
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 5 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    and multiplying out from the left,
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 5 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 5 \\ 1 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix} = A. \checkmark \end{equation*}

    40. Which Matrices Are Elementary?

    1. Which of the following are elementary matrices?
      \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad D = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp -2 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Which of these is always true: the sum, the scalar multiple, the product, or the inverse of elementary matrices is again elementary?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) By Definition 4.49, an elementary matrix comes from an identity matrix by one elementary row operation.
    🔗
    \(A\) is not square, so it is not an elementary matrix at all.
    🔗
    \(B = I_3\) is elementary: multiplying any row of \(I_3\) by the nonzero scalar \(1\) leaves it unchanged.
    🔗
    \(C\) is not: it has a zero row, so it is not invertible, while every elementary matrix is (its operation can be reversed).
    🔗
    \(D\) is: it is \(I_3\) after the single operation \(-2r_3 + r_1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    So the elementary ones are \(B\) and \(D\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) Only the inverse. Undoing one row operation is again one row operation — swap back, multiply by the reciprocal, subtract what was added — so the inverse of an elementary matrix is elementary, as in Example 4.54.
    🔗
    The others all fail. The sum \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}\) differs from \(I\) in four entries. The scalar multiple \(2I\) of the elementary matrix \(I\) comes from \(I\) by two row scalings, not one. And the product of the same two matrices above is \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) again not elementary — indeed Exercise 10.4.37 shows that a general invertible matrix is a product of several elementary matrices, which would be pointless if products stayed elementary.

    which is \(A\) with \(2\) times row \(4\) added to row \(1\text{:}\) the new first row is \((1,-1) + 2(4,-4) = (9,-9)\text{.}\)

    44. ★ Inverting Elementary Matrices by Inspection.

    Without using the inversion algorithm, find \(F^{-1}\) and \(G^{-1}\) for the matrices of Exercise 10.4.42.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The inverse of an elementary matrix is the elementary matrix of the reversing row operation.
    🔗
    \(F\) came from \(4r_2\text{.}\) The operation that undoes it is \(\tfrac{1}{4}r_2\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} F^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp \frac{1}{4} \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \(G\) came from \(2r_4 + r_1\text{.}\) The operation that undoes it is \(-2r_4 + r_1\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} G^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp -2 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Both are again elementary, as Exercise 10.4.40 notes. And \(E\text{,}\) being a row swap, is its own inverse: swapping rows \(1\) and \(3\) twice restores the original.

    45. ★ Factoring a Matrix into Elementary Matrices.

    Express \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 3 \\ 2 \amp 5 \end{bmatrix}\) as a product of elementary matrices, and use the factorization to find \(A^{-1}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Row reduce \(A\) to \(I\text{,}\) recording each operation:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 3 \\ 2 \amp 5 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{-2r_1 + r_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \xrightarrow{-r_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{-3r_2 + r_1} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The corresponding elementary matrices are
    \begin{equation*} E_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ -2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad E_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad E_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    and \(E_3 E_2 E_1 A = I\text{.}\) Two consequences follow at once.
    🔗
    The factorization. Solving for \(A\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} A = E_1^{-1} E_2^{-1} E_3^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Multiplying out from the left confirms it: \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 2 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 3 \\ 2 \amp 5 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) \(\checkmark\)
    🔗
    The inverse. From \(E_3 E_2 E_1 A = I\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} A^{-1} = E_3 E_2 E_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -5 \amp 3 \\ 2 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    This agrees with Theorem 4.36: here \(ad - bc = 5 - 6 = -1\text{,}\) so \(A^{-1} = -\begin{bmatrix} 5 \amp -3 \\ -2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) \(\checkmark\)

    46. Which Identities Always Hold?

    Let \(A\) and \(B\) be \(n \times n\) matrices. Decide whether each identity holds for every such pair, and give a counterexample when it does not.
    1. \((A + B)^T = A^T + B^T\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \((AB)^T = A^T B^T\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\det(A + B^T) = \det(A^T + B)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. If \(AB = 0\) then \(BA = 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. \(AB - BA = 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    6. If \(A^{-1} = B\) then \(AB = BA\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    7. If \(A + B\) is invertible, then \(A\) and \(B\) are both invertible.
      🔗
      🔗
    8. \((A + B)(A - B) = A^2 - B^2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) Always true. Transposition is computed entry by entry, so it respects sums; see Theorem 4.25.
    🔗
    (b) False. The correct rule is \((AB)^T = B^T A^T\) and the order matters. With \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \((AB)^T = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\) while \(A^T B^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (c) Always true. The two matrices are transposes of one another, since \((A + B^T)^T = A^T + B\text{,}\) and a matrix and its transpose have the same determinant by Theorem 6.21.
    🔗
    (d) False. With \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\) we get \(AB = 0\) but \(BA = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \neq 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (e) False — this just says every pair of matrices commutes, which Example 4.19 already refutes.
    🔗
    (f) Always true. If \(B = A^{-1}\) then \(AB = AA^{-1} = I\) and \(BA = A^{-1}A = I\text{,}\) so both equal \(I\text{.}\) This is one of the rare pairs that is guaranteed to commute.
    🔗
    (g) False. Take \(A = I\) and \(B = 0\text{.}\) Then \(A + B = I\) is invertible while \(B\) is not. (Or take \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{:}\) neither is invertible but their sum is \(I\text{.}\))
    🔗
    (h) False. Expanding gives \((A+B)(A-B) = A^2 - AB + BA - B^2\text{,}\) which equals \(A^2 - B^2\) only when \(AB = BA\text{.}\) The pair in (d) provides a counterexample.
    🔗
    🔗

    47. Which Statements Are Equivalent to Invertibility?

    Let \(A\) be a square matrix. Which of the following are equivalent to \(A\) being invertible?
    1. The reduced row-echelon form of \(A\) is \(I\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(A\vec{x} = \vec{b}\) has a unique solution for every \(\vec{b}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(A\vec{x} = \vec{0}\) has a unique solution.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(A\) is a product of elementary matrices.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. \(A\vec{x} = \vec{0}\) has infinitely many solutions.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Statements (a) through (d) are all equivalent to invertibility. Items (a), (b) and (d) appear verbatim in Theorem 4.57. For (c), note that \(\vec{x} = \vec{0}\) is always a solution of the homogeneous system, so “a unique solution” means “only the trivial solution”, which is again on that list.
    🔗
    (e) is not equivalent — it is the exact opposite. If \(A\vec{x} = \vec{0}\) has infinitely many solutions then it has a nontrivial one, and \(A\) is not invertible. For a square matrix these are the only two possibilities: \(A\vec{x} = \vec{0}\) has either only the trivial solution (and \(A\) is invertible) or infinitely many (and it is not).

    48. An Inverse from a Polynomial Equation.

    Suppose the \(n \times n\) matrix \(A\) satisfies \(A^3 - 4A - I = 0\text{.}\) Express \(A^{-1}\) in terms of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Move the identity to the other side and factor out \(A\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} A^3 - 4A = I, \qquad\text{so}\qquad A(A^2 - 4I) = I. \end{equation*}
    The same factoring on the other side gives \((A^2 - 4I)A = I\text{,}\) since powers of \(A\) commute with \(A\text{.}\) So \(A^2 - 4I\) acts as an inverse of \(A\text{,}\) and by uniqueness
    \begin{equation*} A^{-1} = A^2 - 4I. \end{equation*}
    Compare Example 4.62. Note in passing that this argument also proves \(A\) is invertible — we did not have to assume it.

    49. ★ Detecting Non-Invertibility from the Columns.

    Is the following matrix invertible?
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 5 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp 0 \amp 7 \amp 2 \\ 1 \amp 2 \amp 10 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Label the columns \(\vec{u}_1, \vec{u}_2, \vec{u}_3, \vec{u}_4\) and look for a relation. Adding the first two,
    \begin{equation*} \vec{u}_1 + \vec{u}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \vec{u}_4. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    So \(\vec{u}_1 + \vec{u}_2 - \vec{u}_4 = \vec{0}\) is a nontrivial dependence among the columns. The columns are not linearly independent, so the matrix is not invertible by condition (5) of Theorem 4.57.
    🔗
    Equivalently, \(\vec{x} = (1,1,0,-1)\) is a nontrivial solution of \(A\vec{x} = \vec{0}\text{.}\) No row reduction was needed — spotting one relation is enough.

    50. ★ True or False?

    1. If \(A\) is invertible, then so is \(A^T\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \(A\) and \(B\) are invertible \(n \times n\) matrices, then \(A + B\) is invertible.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) True. By Theorem 4.47, \(A^T\) is invertible with \((A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T\text{.}\) The verification is short: \(A^T (A^{-1})^T = (A^{-1}A)^T = I^T = I\text{,}\) and similarly in the other order. This is also proved from scratch in Exercise 10.4.23.
    🔗
    (b) False. Invertibility survives products, not sums. Take \(A = I\) and \(B = -I\text{,}\) both invertible. Then \(A + B = 0\text{,}\) which is not invertible.
    🔗
    A less extreme counterexample: \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(B = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\) are invertible, but \(A + B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix}\) has determinant \(0\text{.}\)

    51. ★ An Inverse from a Polynomial Identity.

    Suppose the square matrix \(P\) satisfies
    \begin{equation*} P^3 - 4P^2 + 5P - 2I = 0. \end{equation*}
    Find \(P^{-1}\text{,}\) if possible.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Isolate the identity and divide by \(2\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} P^3 - 4P^2 + 5P \amp = 2I\\ \tfrac{1}{2}\left( P^3 - 4P^2 + 5P \right) \amp = I. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Every term on the left has a factor of \(P\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} P \left[ \tfrac{1}{2}\left( P^2 - 4P + 5I \right) \right] = I, \end{equation*}
    and by condition (8) of Theorem 4.57, \(P\) is invertible with
    \begin{equation*} P^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left( P^2 - 4P + 5I \right). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Notice that the argument also proves \(P\) is invertible; we never assumed it. The key was that the constant term \(-2I\) is nonzero — had the identity been \(P^3 - 4P^2 + 5P = 0\text{,}\) no such conclusion would follow.
