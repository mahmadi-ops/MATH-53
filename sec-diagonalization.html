    Section 8.2 Multiplicity, Eigenspaces, and Diagonalization

    Subsection 8.2.1 Multiplicity and Basic Eigenvectors

    Definition 8.12. Basic Eigenvector.

    A basic eigenvector of an \(n \times n\) matrix \(A\) is any nonzero multiple of a basic solution to \((\lambda I - A)\vec{x} = 0\text{,}\) where \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.13. An Eigenvalue of Multiplicity Two.

    Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 1 \amp 2 \\ 0 \amp 3 \amp -2 \\ 0 \amp -1 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{align*} \det(\lambda I - A) \amp = \det \begin{bmatrix} \lambda - 4 \amp -1 \amp -2 \\ 0 \amp \lambda - 3 \amp 2 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp \lambda - 2 \end{bmatrix} = (\lambda - 4) \begin{vmatrix} \lambda - 3 \amp 2 \\ 1 \amp \lambda - 2 \end{vmatrix}\\ \amp = (\lambda - 4) \left[ (\lambda - 3)(\lambda - 2) - 2 \right] = (\lambda - 4)\left( \lambda^2 - 5\lambda + 4 \right)\\ \amp = (\lambda - 4)^2 (\lambda - 1). \end{align*}
    We say that \(\lambda = 4\) has multiplicity \(2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For \(\lambda_1 = 4\text{,}\) solving \((4I - A)\vec{x} = \vec{0}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|c} 0 \amp -1 \amp -2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2 \amp 0 \end{array} \right] \longrightarrow \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|c} 0 \amp 1 \amp 2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{array} \right] \; \Longrightarrow \; \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} s \\ -2t \\ t \end{bmatrix} = s \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + t \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Hence the eigenvalue \(\lambda_1 = 4\) has two corresponding basic eigenvectors, \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Note that the basic eigenvectors are NOT scalar multiples of each other.
    🔗
    For \(\lambda_2 = 1\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|c} -3 \amp -1 \amp -2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -2 \amp 2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -1 \amp 0 \end{array} \right] \longrightarrow \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|c} -3 \amp -1 \amp -2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{array} \right] \; \Longrightarrow \; \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} -t \\ t \\ t \end{bmatrix} = t \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    In summary, \(A\) has two eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 = 4\) and \(\lambda_2 = 1\) with multiplicities \(2\) and \(1\) respectively. There are two basic eigenvectors corresponding to \(\lambda_1 = 4\) and one basic eigenvector corresponding to \(\lambda_2 = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Note that any linear combination of basic eigenvectors corresponding to the same eigenvalue is also an eigenvector of \(A\text{.}\) For instance, since
    \begin{equation*} A \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = 4 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \text{and} \qquad A \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = 4 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    it follows that
    \begin{equation*} A \left( 2 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + 3 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right) = 4 \left( 2 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + 3 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.14. Three Distinct Eigenvalues.

    Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of \(B = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp -4 \amp 2 \\ 1 \amp -2 \amp 2 \\ 1 \amp -5 \amp 5 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Expanding \(\det(\lambda I - B)\) and factoring gives
    \begin{equation*} \det(\lambda I - B) = (\lambda - 3)(\lambda^2 - 3\lambda + 2) = (\lambda - 3)(\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 2), \end{equation*}
    so \(\lambda_1 = 1\text{,}\) \(\lambda_2 = 2\text{,}\) \(\lambda_3 = 3\text{.}\) They all have multiplicity one.
    🔗
    Finding the eigenvectors.
    \begin{align*} \lambda_1 = 1: \amp \quad (I - B)\vec{x} = \vec{0} \; \longrightarrow \; \vec{x} = t \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},\\ \lambda_2 = 2: \amp \quad (2I - B)\vec{x} = \vec{0} \; \longrightarrow \; \vec{x} = t \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},\\ \lambda_3 = 3: \amp \quad (3I - B)\vec{x} = \vec{0} \; \longrightarrow \; \vec{x} = \tfrac{t}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{align*}
    So \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) and \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\) are basic eigenvectors corresponding to \(\lambda_1 = 1\text{,}\) \(\lambda_2 = 2\text{,}\) and \(\lambda_3 = 3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.2.2 Eigenspaces

    Note that if an \(n \times n\) matrix \(A\) has \(m\) distinct basic eigenvectors \(\vec{x}_1, \vec{x}_2, \ldots, \vec{x}_m\text{,}\) then, as we have seen in the previous examples, the set \(\{ \vec{x}_1, \vec{x}_2, \ldots, \vec{x}_m \}\) is a linearly independent set (this follows from the definition of a basic eigenvector). Hence \(\operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{x}_1, \ldots, \vec{x}_m \}\) is a subspace.
    🔗
    In fact, since the vectors \(\vec{x}_i\) (\(1 \leq i \leq m\)) are solutions of \((\lambda I - A)\vec{x} = \vec{0}\text{,}\) we know that
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{x}_1, \vec{x}_2, \ldots, \vec{x}_m \} = \operatorname{Nul}(\lambda I - A). \end{equation*}
    Since this set is a subspace of \(\R^n\text{,}\) it is known as the eigenspace of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 8.15. Diagonalizability.

    Let \(A\) be an \(n \times n\) matrix. Then \(A\) is diagonalizable if and only if the matrix \(A\) has \(n\) linearly independent eigenvectors.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Assume that \(A\) has \(n\) linearly independent eigenvectors \(\vec{x}_1, \vec{x}_2, \ldots, \vec{x}_n\text{.}\) Then the matrix \(P = \begin{bmatrix} \vec{x}_1 \amp \vec{x}_2 \amp \cdots \amp \vec{x}_n \end{bmatrix}\) is invertible and we have
    \begin{align*} P^{-1}AP \amp = P^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} A\vec{x}_1 \amp A\vec{x}_2 \amp \cdots \amp A\vec{x}_n \end{bmatrix}\\ \amp = P^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1\vec{x}_1 \amp \lambda_2\vec{x}_2 \amp \cdots \amp \lambda_n\vec{x}_n \end{bmatrix}\\ \amp = P^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} \vec{x}_1 \amp \vec{x}_2 \amp \cdots \amp \vec{x}_n \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 \amp \amp \amp \\ \amp \lambda_2 \amp \amp \\ \amp \amp \ddots \amp \\ \amp \amp \amp \lambda_n \end{bmatrix}\\ \amp = P^{-1}PD = D, \end{align*}
    and hence \(A\) is diagonalizable.
    🔗
    Conversely, if \(A\) is diagonalizable, then there exists an invertible matrix \(P\) such that \(D = P^{-1}AP\) is diagonal, hence \(AP = PD\text{.}\) Now assume \(\vec{x}_1, \vec{x}_2, \ldots, \vec{x}_n\) are columns of \(P\text{.}\) Then comparing columns of \(AP = PD\) gives, for \(i = 1, 2, \ldots, n\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} A\vec{x}_i = \lambda_i \vec{x}_i, \end{equation*}
    which means the diagonal entries of \(D\) are the eigenvalues of \(A\) and the \(\vec{x}_i\) are their corresponding basic eigenvectors, which are linearly independent since we know \(P\) is invertible.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 8.16.

    Revisiting the previous examples: note that \(A\) in Example 8.13 is \(3 \times 3\) and has \(3\) distinct basic eigenvectors. As a result, \(A\) is diagonalizable. Also, notice that \(B\) in Example 8.14 has \(3\) distinct eigenvalues and each eigenvalue has a basic eigenvector corresponding to it, hence \(B\) is diagonalizable as well.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 8.17. Characterizing Diagonalizable Matrices.

    A square matrix \(A\) is diagonalizable if and only if every eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of multiplicity \(m\) yields exactly \(m\) basic eigenvectors, i.e., the solution to \((\lambda I - A)X = 0\) has \(m\) parameters.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 8.18.

    A special case of this result is when every eigenvalue of a matrix has multiplicity one. An \(n \times n\) matrix with \(n\) distinct eigenvalues is diagonalizable.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.2.3 Similar Matrices

    Definition 8.19. Similar Matrices.

    Let \(A\) and \(B\) be \(n \times n\) matrices and suppose there exists an invertible matrix \(P\) such that \(A = P^{-1}BP\text{.}\) Then \(A\) and \(B\) are called similar matrices.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.20. Two Similar Matrices.

    Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 4 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(B = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp 4 \\ 1 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) so that \(P^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} P^{-1}BP = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp 4 \\ 1 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 3 \\ 2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 4 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix} = A, \end{equation*}
    so \(A\) and \(B\) are similar matrices.
    🔗
    Observation. Here \(P\) is obtained from \(I\) by switching the columns, and right-multiplying \(B\) by \(P\) switches the columns of \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 8.21. Similar Matrices Have the Same Eigenvalues.

    Let \(A\) and \(B\) be similar matrices such that \(A = P^{-1}BP\text{,}\) where \(A\text{,}\) \(B\) are \(n \times n\) matrices and \(P\) is invertible. Then \(A\) and \(B\) have the same eigenvalues.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    \begin{align*} \det(\lambda I - A) \amp = \det \left( \lambda I - P^{-1}BP \right) = \det \left( P^{-1}\lambda I P - P^{-1}BP \right)\\ \amp = \det \left( P^{-1}(\lambda I - B)P \right) = \det(P^{-1}) \det(\lambda I - B) \det(P)\\ \amp = \frac{1}{\det(P)} \det(\lambda I - B) \det(P) = \det(\lambda I - B), \end{align*}
    using \(P^{-1}P = I\) in the first line. Hence \(A\) and \(B\) have the same characteristic polynomial and therefore the same eigenvalues.
    🔗
    In particular, if \(A\) is diagonalizable, then \(A\) is similar to a diagonal matrix \(D\text{,}\) and hence the eigenvalues of \(A\) and \(D\) are the same, which means the diagonal entries of \(D\) are the eigenvalues of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.22. Using Similarity to Find Eigenvalues.

    Use the previous theorem to find the eigenvalues of the following matrix:
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 33 \amp 105 \amp 105 \\ 10 \amp 28 \amp 30 \\ -20 \amp -60 \amp -62 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Note that a row operation followed by a column operation produces a much simpler matrix. Applying \(2R_2 + R_3\) (left-multiplying by \(E\)) and then \(-2C_3 + C_2\) (right-multiplying by \(E^{-1}\)):
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 33 \amp 105 \amp 105 \\ 10 \amp 28 \amp 30 \\ -20 \amp -60 \amp -62 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{2R_2 + R_3} \begin{bmatrix} 33 \amp 105 \amp 105 \\ 10 \amp 28 \amp 30 \\ 0 \amp -4 \amp -2 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{-2C_3 + C_2} B = \begin{bmatrix} 33 \amp -105 \amp 105 \\ 10 \amp -32 \amp 30 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp -2 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    where
    \begin{equation*} E = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad E^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus \(B = EAE^{-1}\text{,}\) or \(A = E^{-1}BE\text{,}\) so \(A\) and \(B\) are similar matrices and have the same eigenvalues. Since \(B\) has a row of zeros in its lower-left corner, its characteristic polynomial factors immediately:
    \begin{equation*} \det(\lambda I - A) = \det(\lambda I - B) = -(-2 - \lambda) \begin{vmatrix} 33 - \lambda \amp -105 \\ 10 \amp -32 - \lambda \end{vmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 8.23. Another Addition to the Invertible Matrix Theorem.

    Let \(A\) be an \(n \times n\) matrix. Then \(A\) is invertible if and only if \(0\) is not one of its eigenvalues.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    We know that \(A\) is invertible if and only if \(\det A \neq 0\text{.}\) To prove the above theorem, we need to show that \(\det A \neq 0\) if and only if \(0\) is not one of its eigenvalues.
    🔗
    We first show that \(\det A = \lambda_1 \lambda_2 \cdots \lambda_n\text{,}\) where the \(\lambda_i\) are the eigenvalues of \(A\text{.}\) Remember that the \(\lambda_i\) are roots of the characteristic polynomial, hence
    \begin{equation*} \det(\lambda I - A) = (\lambda - \lambda_1)(\lambda - \lambda_2) \cdots (\lambda - \lambda_n). \end{equation*}
    Now choose \(\lambda = 0\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \det(-A) = (-1)^n \lambda_1 \lambda_2 \cdots \lambda_n. \end{equation*}
    Also, we know \(\det(-A) = (-1)^n \det A\text{.}\) Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \det A = \lambda_1 \lambda_2 \cdots \lambda_n. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Finally, it is clear that if one of the eigenvalues is zero, then \(\det A = 0\text{,}\) and if \(\det A = 0\) then at least one of the eigenvalues must be zero.
