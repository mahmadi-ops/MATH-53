    Section 4.7 Digital Images as Matrices

    Every operation in this chapter was introduced through its arithmetic. In this section we look at the same operations through a different window: a digital photograph is a matrix, so each operation we have defined does something visible to a picture. Scaling a matrix changes the brightness, transposing it reflects the picture across a diagonal, multiplying by a suitable matrix flips or crops it, and multiplying by an inverse undoes a scramble. Nothing new is introduced here; the point is that the arithmetic already developed has content you can see.

    Interactive Sage cells are scattered throughout. Press Evaluate to run one in your browser, or copy the code into the Sage cell server. The cells on this page share their variables, but they must be evaluated in order, so run each one as you reach it.
    🔗

    Subsection 4.7.1 A Photograph Is a Matrix

    A digital image is a rectangular grid of pixels. A colour image records three numbers at each pixel — the intensities of red, green, and blue — so an image that is \(m\) pixels tall and \(n\) pixels wide is stored as three \(m \times n\) matrices \(R\text{,}\) \(G\text{,}\) and \(B\text{.}\) Each entry is an integer between \(0\) and \(255\text{,}\) where \(0\) means none of that colour and \(255\) means as much as the display can produce.
    🔗
    To convert the image to grayscale we must replace the three numbers at each pixel by a single number recording how bright that pixel is. The standard choice is the weighted average
    \begin{equation*} M = 0.299\,R + 0.587\,G + 0.114\,B, \end{equation*}
    with the resulting entries rounded to integers. Read that formula again with the chapter in mind: it is a linear combination of three matrices, built from exactly the scalar multiplication and addition of Matrix Operations. The weights are unequal because the human eye is far more sensitive to green light than to blue, so an unweighted average \(\frac{1}{3}(R + G + B)\) would render greens too dark and blues too light.
    🔗
    The grayscale image in 4.63(b) is \(780\) pixels tall and \(520\) pixels wide, so \(M\) is a \(780 \times 520\) matrix with \(405{,}600\) entries — far too large to print. But nothing stops us from looking at a piece of it. A submatrix of \(M\text{,}\) obtained by keeping some of its rows and some of its columns, is exactly a rectangular patch of the picture. The patch around the cat’s left eye — rows \(297\) through \(376\) and columns \(231\) through \(290\) — is an \(80 \times 60\) submatrix, which is still \(4800\) numbers. So we shrink it: cut the patch into \(5 \times 5\) blocks of pixels and replace each block by the average of its \(25\) entries. Averaging blocks like this is how an image is reduced to a lower resolution, and it leaves the \(16 \times 12\) matrix
    \begin{equation*} D = \begin{bmatrix} 74 \amp 66 \amp 53 \amp 52 \amp 73 \amp 78 \amp 80 \amp 82 \amp 82 \amp 78 \amp 82 \amp 77 \\ 62 \amp 56 \amp 65 \amp 84 \amp 83 \amp 87 \amp 83 \amp 89 \amp 84 \amp 80 \amp 84 \amp 74 \\ 73 \amp 67 \amp 84 \amp 87 \amp 87 \amp 89 \amp 92 \amp 89 \amp 91 \amp 86 \amp 89 \amp 81 \\ 70 \amp 80 \amp 85 \amp 87 \amp 91 \amp 91 \amp 91 \amp 82 \amp 94 \amp 90 \amp 91 \amp 86 \\ 67 \amp 84 \amp 87 \amp 92 \amp 97 \amp 95 \amp 95 \amp 82 \amp 95 \amp 91 \amp 85 \amp 86 \\ 83 \amp 90 \amp 92 \amp 87 \amp 75 \amp 76 \amp 91 \amp 93 \amp 95 \amp 91 \amp 86 \amp 86 \\ 90 \amp 90 \amp 80 \amp 47 \amp 70 \amp 75 \amp 55 \amp 87 \amp 95 \amp 93 \amp 88 \amp 90 \\ 87 \amp 88 \amp 54 \amp 89 \amp 123 \amp 36 \amp 76 \amp 59 \amp 96 \amp 95 \amp 94 \amp 91 \\ 95 \amp 106 \amp 60 \amp 116 \amp 103 \amp 27 \amp 76 \amp 74 \amp 92 \amp 94 \amp 96 \amp 89 \\ 99 \amp 111 \amp 81 \amp 108 \amp 133 \amp 49 \amp 120 \amp 78 \amp 84 \amp 95 \amp 94 \amp 86 \\ 101 \amp 107 \amp 109 \amp 72 \amp 115 \amp 132 \amp 104 \amp 48 \amp 88 \amp 102 \amp 101 \amp 83 \\ 93 \amp 100 \amp 104 \amp 111 \amp 97 \amp 86 \amp 94 \amp 86 \amp 99 \amp 106 \amp 105 \amp 90 \\ 91 \amp 94 \amp 97 \amp 102 \amp 101 \amp 99 \amp 104 \amp 95 \amp 118 \amp 120 \amp 111 \amp 91 \\ 84 \amp 91 \amp 91 \amp 98 \amp 105 \amp 110 \amp 116 \amp 85 \amp 85 \amp 83 \amp 78 \amp 62 \\ 79 \amp 94 \amp 98 \amp 100 \amp 103 \amp 115 \amp 118 \amp 79 \amp 60 \amp 79 \amp 57 \amp 51 \\ 79 \amp 84 \amp 92 \amp 85 \amp 109 \amp 113 \amp 116 \amp 116 \amp 76 \amp 64 \amp 53 \amp 90 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    shown magnified in 4.64. Each entry is now one square of the picture: the small numbers are the dark squares and the large ones are the light squares. The \(27\) in row \(9\) is the darkest part of the pupil, and the \(133\) in the row below it is the brightest.

    Subsection 4.7.2 Scalar Multiplication and Addition: Brightness and Negatives

    Multiplying \(M\) by a scalar \(c > 1\) raises every intensity by the same factor and so brightens the picture, while \(0 \lt c \lt 1\) darkens it. There is a wrinkle: an intensity above \(255\) is not a colour any display can show, so after computing \(cM\) the values above \(255\) must be replaced by \(255\text{.}\) That last step is not a matrix operation, and it destroys information — several distinct bright values collapse to the same white, which is why an overexposed photograph cannot be repaired by darkening it.
    🔗
    Addition is just as visible. Let \(J\) be the \(m \times n\) matrix all of whose entries are \(1\text{,}\) so that \(255J\) is a completely white image. Then
    \begin{equation*} N = 255J - M \end{equation*}
    replaces each intensity \(v\) by \(255 - v\text{,}\) turning black into white and white into black. This is the photographic negative, and it needs no clipping: if \(0 \leq v \leq 255\) then \(0 \leq 255 - v \leq 255\) automatically.
    🔗
    Both operations are worth trying on the submatrix \(D\text{.}\) In Sage, D.nrows() and D.ncols() report the dimensions of \(D\text{,}\) and ones_matrix builds the matrix \(J\) of that same size.
    🔗
    Applying the operation twice returns the original, since \(255J - (255J - M) = M\text{.}\) Replace N by 255*J - N in the cell above and evaluate again to watch the second negative undo the first.

    Subsection 4.7.3 Transposition: Reflecting Across the Diagonal

    Transposition interchanges the roles of the row index and the column index, so the pixel in row \(i\) and column \(j\) of \(M^T\) is the pixel in row \(j\) and column \(i\) of \(M\text{.}\) Geometrically, the picture is reflected across the main diagonal, the line running from the upper-left corner to the lower-right corner. Note also that a tall \(m \times n\) image becomes a wide \(n \times m\) one.
    🔗
    A reflection is not a rotation, and it is easy to conflate the two. Turning the page to look at 4.66 sideways does not recover the original picture: the reflected image is also mirrored. We will see in Matrix Multiplication: Flips and Crops that a genuine rotation requires a transposition and a flip.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.7.4 Matrix Multiplication: Flips and Crops

    Multiplication is the operation whose visual meaning is least obvious, so we build it up from the identity matrix. Since \(I_m M = M\) and \(M I_n = M\text{,}\) multiplying by an identity matrix leaves the picture alone. Now permute the columns of the identity: let \(K_n\) be the \(n \times n\) matrix obtained from \(I_n\) by writing its columns in the reverse order,
    \begin{equation*} K_4 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Multiplying on the right by \(K_n\) reverses the order of the columns of \(M\text{,}\) and multiplying on the left by \(K_m\) reverses the order of its rows. In pictures, \(M K_n\) is the mirror image of \(M\) and \(K_m M\) is \(M\) turned upside down.
    🔗

    Remark 4.67.

    Here is Matrix Multiplication made visible: the products \(K M\) and \(M K\) are genuinely different pictures, one flipped top to bottom and the other left to right. Multiplication does not commute, and which side you multiply on decides what happens.
    🔗
    🔗
    With both flips available we can say precisely what a rotation is. Reflecting across the diagonal and then reversing the order of the columns gives
    \begin{equation*} M^T K_m, \end{equation*}
    which is \(M\) rotated a quarter turn clockwise. So a rotation is a transposition followed by a flip, exactly as promised in Transposition: Reflecting Across the Diagonal.
    🔗
    Cropping is the same idea with a non-square factor. Delete rows from \(I_m\) to leave a \(k \times m\) matrix \(E\text{,}\) keeping only the rows of the identity that correspond to the rows of the picture you want; then \(EM\) is the \(k \times n\) matrix consisting of just those rows. Doing the same on the right selects columns, so a rectangular crop is a product \(EMF\text{.}\) This is how the \(80 \times 60\) patch around the eye in A Photograph Is a Matrix is cut out of \(M\text{:}\) by multiplication alone, with no need to speak of indices at all.
    🔗
    The reversal matrix has a \(1\) exactly where \(i + j = n - 1\text{,}\) which is what the rule below builds. (Sage indexes rows and columns from zero.) Evaluate the cell and compare the four pictures: the original, the two flips, and the quarter turn.

    Subsection 4.7.5 Inverses: Scrambling a Picture and Putting It Back

    The reversal matrix \(K_n\) is one example of a permutation matrix: a matrix obtained from an identity matrix by rearranging its rows. Every such matrix is a product of the row-swap elementary matrices of Elementary Matrices, and so is invertible. If \(P\) is an \(m \times m\) permutation matrix, then \(PM\) is the picture with its rows of pixels shuffled into a new order — unrecognizable, if the shuffle is thorough.
    🔗
    Because \(P\) is invertible, no information has been lost. By condition (7) of Theorem 4.57, each column of the scrambled picture came from exactly one column of the original, so the original can be recovered as
    \begin{equation*} P^{-1}(PM) = (P^{-1}P)M = I_m M = M. \end{equation*}
    Better still, a permutation matrix satisfies \(P^{-1} = P^T\text{,}\) so the inverse costs nothing to write down: it is the transpose. Shuffling the rows and unshuffling them are the same operation performed with \(P\) and with \(P^T\text{.}\)

    Remark 4.70.

    It is tempting to call this encryption, and in a very weak sense it is: \(P\) is the key, \(PM\) is the ciphertext, and \(P^T\) is the decryption key. But no pixel has been changed, only moved, so the shuffled image has exactly the same collection of rows as the original and is easily unscrambled by anyone willing to match up the edges. The mathematics that matters here is not the secrecy: it is that an invertible matrix loses nothing, and a non-invertible one does. Multiplying by a matrix that is not invertible — averaging neighbouring rows, say, or discarding every second one — also produces a new picture, but there is then no matrix that undoes it.
