    Section 9.2 Long-Run Behaviour: Two More Examples

    When a matrix is diagonalizable, writing the initial state in a basis of eigenvectors makes the long-run behaviour transparent: the term belonging to the largest eigenvalue dominates, and everything else decays.
    🔗

    Example 9.16. Truck Rentals.

    A truck rental company has \(3\) locations, where you can rent moving trucks. You can return them to any other location. For simplicity, suppose that every customer returns their truck the next day. Let \(V_k\) be the vector whose entries are the number of trucks in locations \(1\text{,}\) \(2\text{,}\) and \(3\text{,}\) respectively. Let \(A\) be the matrix whose \((i,j)\)-entry is the probability that a customer renting a truck from location \(j\) returns it to location \(i\text{.}\) Consider
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.3 \amp 0.4 \amp 0.5 \\ 0.3 \amp 0.4 \amp 0.3 \\ 0.4 \amp 0.2 \amp 0.2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Assume that initially the trucks are distributed in the \(3\) locations as \(\vec{V}_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 3 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) and find out the percentages of trucks that will be distributed in each location in the long run.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Expanding \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\) and factoring gives
    \begin{equation*} -(\lambda + 0.2)(1 - \lambda)(0.1 - \lambda) = 0, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \lambda_1 = 1, \quad \lambda_2 = -0.2, \quad \lambda_3 = 0.1. \end{equation*}
    Here \(\lambda_1 = 1\) is the dominant eigenvalue.
    🔗
    For \(\lambda_1 = 1\text{:}\) row reducing \((A - I)\vec{x} = \vec{0}\) gives
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{7}{5}t \\ \frac{6}{5}t \\ t \end{bmatrix} = \frac{t}{5} \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 6 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \vec{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 6 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Similarly, for the other eigenvalues we find
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \text{ for } \lambda_2 = -0.2, \qquad \vec{x}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \text{ for } \lambda_3 = 0.1. \end{equation*}
    The set \(\{ \vec{x}_1, \vec{x}_2, \vec{x}_3 \}\) is linearly independent since the \(\vec{x}_i\) are eigenvectors of \(A\) for distinct eigenvalues. Hence we can write \(\vec{V}_0 = a_1\vec{x}_1 + a_2\vec{x}_2 + a_3\vec{x}_3\) and
    \begin{align*} A^k \vec{V}_0 \amp = A^k \left( a_1\vec{x}_1 + a_2\vec{x}_2 + a_3\vec{x}_3 \right)\\ \amp = a_1 \left( A^k \vec{x}_1 \right) + a_2 \left( A^k \vec{x}_2 \right) + a_3 \left( A^k \vec{x}_3 \right)\\ \amp = a_1 \lambda_1^k \vec{x}_1 + a_2 \lambda_2^k \vec{x}_2 + a_3 \lambda_3^k \vec{x}_3. \end{align*}
    🔗
    (I) When \(k \to \infty\text{,}\) the dominant term is \(a_1 \lambda_1^k \vec{x}_1\text{,}\) since \(|\lambda_2|, |\lambda_3| \lt 1\) and so those terms decay to zero.
    🔗
    (II) In this problem \(\vec{V}_0 = \vec{x}_1 + \vec{x}_2 + \vec{x}_3\text{,}\) which means \(a_1 = 1\text{.}\) Therefore
    \begin{equation*} A^k \vec{V}_0 \; \longrightarrow \; (1)(1)^k \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 6 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 6 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \text{as } k \to \infty. \end{equation*}
    In the long run the \(18\) trucks distribute themselves as \(7\text{,}\) \(6\text{,}\) and \(5\) among the three locations.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.17. An Owl Population.

    Consider an owl population of \(100\) adult females and \(40\) juvenile females. As biologists, we wish to study the growth of the population having determined the following.
    1. The number of juvenile females hatched in any year is twice the number of adult females in the previous year.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Half the adult females in any year survive to the next year.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. One quarter of the juvenile females in any year survive to adulthood.
      🔗
      🔗
    Will the owls survive?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(a_n\) be the number of adult females after \(n\) years and let \(j_n\) be the number of juvenile females after \(n\) years. The three conditions say
    \begin{equation*} j_{n+1} = 2a_n, \qquad a_{n+1} = \frac{a_n}{2} + \frac{j_n}{4}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Writing the state vector after \(n\) years as \(V_n = \begin{bmatrix} a_n \\ j_n \end{bmatrix}\) with \(V_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 100 \\ 40 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) we get
    \begin{equation*} V_{n+1} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{a_n}{2} + \frac{j_n}{4} \\ 2a_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{2} \amp \frac{1}{4} \\ 2 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a_n \\ j_n \end{bmatrix} = A V_n. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The matrix \(A = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{2} \amp \frac{1}{4} \\ 2 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\) has two eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 = 1\) and \(\lambda_2 = -\frac{1}{2}\text{,}\) with eigenvectors \(\vec{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\vec{x}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Hence
    \begin{equation*} D = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -\frac{1}{2} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \\ 2 \amp 4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad P^{-1} = \frac{1}{6} \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 1 \\ -2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} V_n = A^n V_0 = P D^n P^{-1} V_0 = \frac{1}{6} \begin{bmatrix} 440 + 160 \left( -\frac{1}{2} \right)^n \\ 880 - 640 \left( -\frac{1}{2} \right)^n \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We can also approximate \(V_n\) directly from the dominant eigenvalue. Writing \(\begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \end{bmatrix} = P^{-1}V_0\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} b_1 = \frac{1}{6} \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 100 \\ 40 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{220}{3}, \end{equation*}
    so
    \begin{equation*} V_n \approx b_1 \lambda_1^n \vec{x}_1 = \left( \frac{220}{3} \right) (1)^n \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{220}{3} \\ \frac{440}{3} \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    which agrees with our previous result as \(n \to \infty\text{.}\)
    🔗
    So the owls survive: since \(\lambda_1 = 1\text{,}\) the population neither grows without bound nor dies out, but settles at roughly \(73\) adult and \(147\) juvenile females.
