    Section 10.5 Orthogonal Least Squares

    A system \(A\vec{x} = \vec{b}\) with more equations than unknowns is usually inconsistent: the measured \(\vec{b}\) does not lie in \(\operatorname{Col} A\text{.}\) The method of least squares asks instead for the \(\vec{x}\) that makes \(A\vec{x}\) as close to \(\vec{b}\) as possible, and orthogonal projection answers the question completely. This section derives the normal equations, solves them efficiently with the \(QR\) factorization, and applies the method to the problem that motivates it in practice: fitting a line or curve to data.
    🔗

    Subsection 10.5.1 Inconsistent Systems and the Least-Squares Problem

    Introduces the setting with a small overdetermined system that has no solution, and defines a least-squares solution as a vector \(\hat{\vec{x}}\) minimizing \(\| \vec{b} - A\vec{x} \|\) over all \(\vec{x} \in \R^n\text{.}\) Since \(A\vec{x}\) ranges over \(\operatorname{Col} A\text{,}\) the closest point is the projection \(\vec{b}_{\parallel}\) of \(\vec{b}\) onto \(\operatorname{Col} A\text{,}\) and a least-squares solution is any \(\hat{\vec{x}}\) with \(A\hat{\vec{x}} = \vec{b}_{\parallel}\text{.}\) The minimal error is \(\| \vec{b}_{\perp} \|\text{.}\) An interactive figure shows \(\vec{b}\) hovering above the plane \(\operatorname{Col} A\) in \(\R^3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 10.5.2 The Normal Equations

    Uses \((\operatorname{Col} A)^{\perp} = \operatorname{Nul} A^T\) to convert \(\vec{b} - A\hat{\vec{x}} \in (\operatorname{Col} A)^{\perp}\) into the normal equations \(A^TA\,\hat{\vec{x}} = A^T\vec{b}\text{,}\) a square system that is always consistent. Proves that \(A^TA\) is invertible exactly when the columns of \(A\) are linearly independent, in which case the least-squares solution is unique, \(\hat{\vec{x}} = (A^TA)^{-1}A^T\vec{b}\text{,}\) and the projection matrix onto \(\operatorname{Col} A\) is \(P = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T\text{.}\) Worked examples solve the normal equations by row reduction and check that the residual \(\vec{b} - A\hat{\vec{x}}\) is orthogonal to every column of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 10.5.3 Least Squares via the \(QR\) Factorization

    Substitutes \(A = QR\) into the normal equations and uses \(Q^TQ = I\) to reduce them to \(R\hat{\vec{x}} = Q^T\vec{b}\text{,}\) an upper triangular system solved by back substitution with no matrix inverse in sight. Compares the two routes on the same example, and notes that when the columns of \(A\) are orthonormal already the answer is simply \(\hat{\vec{x}} = A^T\vec{b}\text{,}\) which is the projection formula in disguise.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 10.5.4 Fitting a Line to Data

    Given data points \((t_1, y_1), \ldots, (t_m, y_m)\text{,}\) seeks the line \(y = c_0 + c_1 t\) that best fits them and shows this is the least-squares problem \(A\vec{c} = \vec{y}\) with \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp t_1 \\ \vdots \amp \vdots \\ 1 \amp t_m \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Explains what “best” means (minimizing the sum of squared vertical residuals), works a complete example by hand, and provides a Sage cell that fits and plots a line through a real data set. Interprets the residual vector and the quantity \(\| \vec{y}_{\perp} \|\) as a measure of how well the line explains the data.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 10.5.5 Beyond Lines: Fitting Curves and Surfaces

    Observes that nothing in the previous subsection used the fact that the model was a line: any model that is linear in its unknown coefficients — a quadratic \(y = c_0 + c_1 t + c_2 t^2\text{,}\) a sum of sines and cosines, or a function of several input variables — leads to a least-squares problem with a different design matrix \(A\text{.}\) Fits a parabola and a periodic model to sample data, connects the polynomial case back to the interpolation problem of Section 1.5 (exact fit when there are as many parameters as data points, least squares when there are fewer), and closes with a caution about overfitting.
