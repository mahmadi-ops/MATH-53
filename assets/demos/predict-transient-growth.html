Why the peak stays finite

Write r = 2/3 and s = 2/3 − ε. For ε > 0, the distinct eigenvalues r and s have eigenvectors (1, 0) and (6, −ε), so the matrix is diagonalizable. At ε = 0 these directions coalesce, and there is only one independent eigenvector.

For n ≥ 1,

ε > 0: xn = (6(rn − sn)/ε, sn);
ε = 0: xn = (6nrn−1, rn).

Both coordinates tend to zero, even at the defective endpoint. Yet M0 = 1 and M1 = 6: convergence can begin with substantial growth.

If un is the first coordinate, then u1 = 6, u2 = 8 − 6ε, and u3 − u2 = −6ε(1 − ε). Also un+1 − un = −un/3 + 6sn. Once this difference is nonpositive, the next is strictly negative: it equals r times the preceding difference plus 6(s − 1)sn. The second coordinate never exceeds 1.

Thus the exact peak is 8 − 6ε at n = 2. At ε = 0 it is also attained at n = 3; at ε = 1/3 it is also attained at n = 1. As ε → 0, the peak tends to 8. A coalescing eigenbasis does not force this trajectory’s peak to become unbounded.
