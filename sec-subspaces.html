    Section 5.1 Subspaces

    Some subsets of \(\R^n\) are closed under the two operations we care about: adding two of their vectors, or scaling one, never takes you outside the subset. These are the subspaces, and they turn out to be exactly the spans.
    🔗

    Subsection 5.1.1 Definition and Examples

    Definition 5.1. Subspace.

    Let \(H\) be a nonempty collection of vectors in \(\R^n\text{.}\) Then \(H\) is a subspace if whenever \(a\) and \(b\) are scalars and \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\) are vectors in \(H\text{,}\) \(a\vec{u} + b\vec{v}\) is also in \(H\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.2. Which of These Is a Subspace?

    Among the collection of vectors described below, which one is a subspace of \(\R^n\text{?}\)
    🔗
    1. A plane through the origin in \(\R^3\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. A line not containing the origin in \(\R^2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (A) Yes, this is a subspace.
    1. This subset of \(\R^3\) contains the zero vector.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If two vectors are on this plane, so is any linear combination of them.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    (B) No, this is not a subspace. Note that, for example, \(\vec{u}\) is in the subset of vectors drawn along the line, however \(-\vec{u}\) is not. Hence this subset of vectors in \(\R^2\) is NOT a subspace of \(\R^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.5. True or False?

    Any subspace \(H\) of \(\R^n\) contains the zero vector.
    🔗
    Solution.
    This is true. Note that since a subspace is nonempty, it has at least one element \(\vec{u}\text{.}\) But then \(0\vec{u} = \vec{0}\) is in \(H\) since \(H\) is closed under scalar multiplication.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.6. A Set That Misses the Origin.

    Is the set \(T = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} x - 1 \\ x + 1 \end{bmatrix}, \; x \in \R \right\}\) a subspace of \(\R^2\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    No. Clearly \(\vec{0} \notin T\text{,}\) since \(\begin{bmatrix} x-1 \\ x+1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\) is an inconsistent system: it would need \(x = 1\) and \(x = -1\) at once.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.7. A Line through the Origin Is a Subspace.

    Is the set \(T = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} t \; : \; t \in \R \right\}\) a subspace of \(\R^3\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Yes.
    🔗
    (I) Choose \(t = 0\) to see that \(\vec{0} \in T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (II) For any \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\) in \(T\text{,}\) \(a\vec{u} + b\vec{v}\) is also in \(T\text{.}\) If \(\vec{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} t_1\) and \(\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} t_2\text{,}\) then
    \begin{align*} a\vec{u} + b\vec{v} \amp = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} a t_1 + \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} b t_2\\ \amp = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \left( a t_1 + b t_2 \right)\\ \amp = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} t_3, \qquad \text{where } t_3 \in \R. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.8. Examples of Subspaces of \(\R^3\).

    (I) \(H_1 = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} \in \R^3 : x = y = z \right\}\text{.}\) Note that this is a line that passes through the origin and can be thought of as \(H_1 = \operatorname{Span}\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (II) \(H_2 = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} \in \R^3 : x - y = 0 \right\}\text{.}\) Note that this is a plane that passes through the origin and can be thought of as \(H_2 = \operatorname{Span}\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (III)
    \begin{equation*} H_3 = \R^3 = \operatorname{Span}\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\} = \operatorname{Span}\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \right\}, \end{equation*}
    since
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \left( \frac{x+y}{2} \right) \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \left( \frac{x-y}{2} \right) \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + z \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.1.2 Subspaces Are Spans

    Theorem 5.9. Subspaces Are Spans and Spans Are Subspaces.

    Let \(H\) be a nonempty collection of vectors in \(\R^n\text{.}\) Then \(H\) is a subspace of \(\R^n\) if and only if there exist vectors \(\{ \vec{u}_1, \ldots, \vec{u}_k \}\) in \(H\) such that
    \begin{equation*} H = \operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{u}_1, \ldots, \vec{u}_k \}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    We only show that if \(H\) is a subspace, then \(H = \operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{u}_1, \ldots, \vec{u}_k \}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Pick a vector \(\vec{u}_1 \in H\text{.}\) If \(H = \operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{u}_1 \}\text{,}\) then we are done. If \(H \neq \operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{u}_1 \}\text{,}\) then there exists another vector \(\vec{u}_2 \in H\) which is not in \(\operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{u}_1 \}\text{.}\) Now consider \(\operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{u}_1, \vec{u}_2 \}\text{.}\) If \(H = \operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{u}_1, \vec{u}_2 \}\text{,}\) then we are done. Otherwise, pick \(\vec{u}_3 \notin \operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{u}_1, \vec{u}_2 \}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If we continue this way, the process must stop with \(\vec{u}_k\text{,}\) where \(k \leq n\text{,}\) and thus \(H = \operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{u}_1, \ldots, \vec{u}_k \}\text{.}\) The process must terminate because, as we discussed in Theorem 2.39, in \(\R^n\text{,}\) if \(k \gt n\) then the set \(\{ \vec{u}_1, \vec{u}_2, \ldots, \vec{u}_k \}\) is linearly dependent.
    🔗
    🔗

    Corollary 5.10. Subspaces Are Spans of Linearly Independent Vectors.

    If \(H\) is a subspace of \(\R^n\text{,}\) then there exist linearly independent vectors \(\{ \vec{u}_1, \ldots, \vec{u}_k \}\) of \(H\) such that
    \begin{equation*} H = \operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{u}_1, \ldots, \vec{u}_k \}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Since the vectors \(\vec{u}_i\) we picked in the proof above are not in the span of the previous vectors, they must be linearly independent.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.11. Recognizing a Subspace from a Parametrization.

    Is the set \(V = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} a - 3b \\ 2a + b \\ a \end{bmatrix}, \; a, b \in \R \right\}\) a subspace of \(\R^3\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Yes, since
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} a - 3b \\ 2a + b \\ a \end{bmatrix} = a \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + b \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    which means \(V = \operatorname{Span}\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \right\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hence, from Theorem 5.9, we can conclude that \(V\) is a subspace of \(\R^3\text{.}\) The set \(\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \right\}\) is a linearly independent spanning set for \(V\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Note that \(V\) can also be written as
    \begin{equation*} V = \operatorname{Span}\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}, \end{equation*}
    where this spanning set is not linearly independent, since \(\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
