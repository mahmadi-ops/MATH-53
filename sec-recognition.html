    Section 7.7 Finding the Cat: A First Look at Image Recognition

    In Digital Images as Matrices we did arithmetic to pictures, and in A Plane of Cats: Linear Combinations of Images we added and scaled them. This section puts that arithmetic to work on a question of a new kind: given a small patch of pixels, decide whether it was cut from the corner of the box where the cat’s eye is, or from the same corner of the other photograph, where there is nothing but wall (7.45). The machine built here to decide it will not be clever. It will subtract, square, add, and compare—and that will be enough to recognize the eye through camera grain that all but buries it.
    🔗
    Everything is built from tools already on the table: pictures as vectors and averages of pictures from A Plane of Cats: Linear Combinations of Images, coordinates from Coordinates and Change of Basis, linear transformations from Linear Transformations and Isomorphisms, and one import from An Application: The Determinant as Volume: the dot product. The result is a first step, not a finished subject, and the section ends with the failures as well as the successes. The failures are the more instructive half.
    🔗
    As in A Plane of Cats: Linear Combinations of Images, interactive Sage cells appear throughout, the cells on this page share their variables, and each should be evaluated as you reach it. The lines marked # change me are the ones worth experimenting with. Each cell is also described in words, so that the argument can be followed with no cell evaluated at all.
    🔗
    A handful of symbols carry the whole story, and each is defined where it first appears. 7.46 collects them in one place to return to.
    🔗

    Subsection 7.7.1 The Distance Between Two Pictures

    Recognition, as this section practices it, is comparison: the machine remembers what each corner looks like and asks which memory the new patch resembles more. So the first thing needed is a number that measures how far apart two pictures are. The dot product of Definition 6.27 was stated in \(\R^3\text{,}\) but its formula—multiply matching coordinates, add— never uses the fact that there are three of them. Word for word, the same recipe runs on any number of coordinates, and a picture in \(\mathbb{M}_{mn}\text{,}\) read through its pixel entries, has \(mn\) coordinates to offer it.
    🔗
    It is worth naming what is being used here. Reading a picture through its pixel entries—listing them in a fixed order, say row by row—is the coordinate map of Coordinates and Change of Basis taken with respect to the pixel basis \(\mathcal{E} = \{ E_{ij} \}\) of A Plane of Cats: Linear Combinations of Images, and that map is an isomorphism
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{M}_{mn} \;\longrightarrow\; \R^{mn}, \qquad X \longmapsto [X]_{\mathcal{E}}. \end{equation*}
    By Remark 7.14, then, \(\mathbb{M}_{mn}\) and \(\R^{mn}\) are the same space wearing different clothes: a \(780 \times 520\) photograph is a vector in \(\R^{405{,}600}\) that happens to be printed in a rectangle. So the definition that follows invents nothing. It is the dot product we already have on \(\R^{mn}\text{,}\) carried back to pictures along that isomorphism—which is also why every fact proved about it in \(\R^n\) remains true here.
    🔗

    Definition 7.47. Dot Product, Length, and Distance for Pictures.

    For pictures \(X = [x_{ij}]\) and \(Y = [y_{ij}]\) in \(\mathbb{M}_{mn}\text{,}\) the dot product, the length, and the distance are
    \begin{equation*} X \cdot Y = \sum_{i,j} x_{ij}\, y_{ij}, \qquad \| X \| = \sqrt{X \cdot X}, \qquad \operatorname{dist}(X, Y) = \| X - Y \|. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Unwinding the last of these gives the comparison rule in its most concrete form, the one the machine will actually execute:
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{dist}(X, Y) = \sqrt{ \sum_{i,j} \left( x_{ij} - y_{ij} \right)^2 }. \end{equation*}
    Subtract the two pictures pixel by pixel, square each difference, add up the squares, take the square root. It is the root-sum-square of the disagreements at corresponding pixels, and nothing more.
    🔗
    Two properties can be read directly off the formula and will be used without comment: the dot product is symmetric, \(X \cdot Y = Y \cdot X\text{,}\) and it is linear in each argument, so that expressions like \((X + Y) \cdot Z\) and \((cX) \cdot Z\) expand exactly as ordinary products do. One consequence is worth displaying now, because it will be used twice:
    \begin{equation} \| X - Y \|^2 = \| X \|^2 - 2\, X \cdot Y + \| Y \|^2.\tag{7.1} \end{equation}
    Two more consequences give the distance its meaning. Since \(\| X - Y \|^2\) is a sum of squares, it is zero exactly when every term is zero: the distance between two pictures is \(0\) precisely when they agree at every pixel. And \(\| cX \|^2 = (cX) \cdot (cX) = c^2 \| X \|^2\text{,}\) so scaling a picture by \(c\) scales its length by \(|c|\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Try Definition 7.47 on a \(2 \times 2\) example. Although the pictures
    \begin{equation*} U = \begin{bmatrix} 90 \amp 60 \\ 120 \amp 30 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad V = \begin{bmatrix} 86 \amp 63 \\ 120 \amp 30 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    are \(2 \times 2\) matrices, we can regard them as points in \(\R^4\text{,}\) with one coordinate for each pixel. Their difference is
    \begin{equation*} [U - V]_{\mathcal{E}} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ -3 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{dist}(U, V) = \sqrt{4^2 + (-3)^2 + 0^2 + 0^2} = 5. \end{equation*}
    Since only two coordinates differ, this is precisely the Pythagorean theorem applied to a \(3\)-\(4\)-\(5\) right triangle inside the four-dimensional space of \(2 \times 2\) pictures.
    🔗
    The pictures this section will recognize are the two blocks of The Arithmetic Behind a Blend: the patch \(A_0\) around the alive cat’s left eye, and the patch \(D_0\) cut from the same corner of the dead-cat photograph, where the wall is bare—the rectangle marked in 7.45. Enter them, together with a labelled version of the drawing helper from that section and the distance just defined. The cell prints the distance between the two corners and draws them side by side, each panel titled. Every later cell on this page depends on the names defined here, so evaluate this cell first—and evaluate it again if you reload the page or return after a long pause, since either starts a fresh session that has forgotten them.
    🔗
    The two corners sit \(\sqrt{15678} \approx 125.2\) units apart. That one number is the yardstick for everything that follows: it is the distance a patch would have to travel to go from being one corner to being the other, and every verdict the machine renders will amount to asking which side of that journey a patch is on.
    🔗

    Question 7.48. Whose Coordinates Does the Distance Use?

    The formula in Definition 7.47 reads a picture through its pixel entries—its coordinates with respect to the pixel basis \(\mathcal{E}\) of A Plane of Cats: Linear Combinations of Images. But 7.41 showed the same picture wearing different coordinates in a different basis. If we computed the same formula from those coordinates instead, would we get the same length?
    🔗
    Answer.
    No. Inside the plane of cats of A Plane of Cats: Linear Combinations of Images, the picture \(A\) has \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinates \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) whose formula gives \(1\text{,}\) and \(\mathcal{C}\)-coordinates \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) whose formula gives \(\sqrt{2}\text{;}\) and the \(405{,}600\) pixel entries of the photograph itself give yet a third, vastly larger number. The formula does not read the vector; it reads the list of coordinates it is handed, so the number it returns depends on which basis produced that list.
     2 
    Stated carefully, a length comes from a choice of measurement rather than from a choice of basis. The formula of Definition 7.47 is one such choice, known as the Frobenius inner product on \(\mathbb{M}_{mn}\text{;}\) the pixel basis is orthonormal for it, and so is any other orthonormal basis, all of which return the same length. An arbitrary basis—such as \(\mathcal{B}\) or \(\mathcal{C}\) above—does not. Inner products and orthonormality belong to the course that follows this one; here it is enough to fix one basis and never move.
    Throughout this section that basis is fixed once and for all: the pixel basis, one coordinate per pixel, which is also exactly the choice under which Definition 7.47 agrees with Definition 6.27 in \(\R^3\text{.}\)

    Subsection 7.7.2 Recognition by Nearest Reference

    The machine can now be stated in one sentence.
    🔗

    Definition 7.49. The Nearest-Reference Rule.

    Fix reference pictures \(P\) and \(Q\text{,}\) each carrying a label. Given a new picture \(X\text{,}\) compute \(\operatorname{dist}(X, P)\) and \(\operatorname{dist}(X, Q)\text{,}\) and assign \(X\) the label of the nearer reference. If the two distances are equal, the rule declines to answer.
    🔗
    🔗
    Take \(P = A_0\) with the label cat and \(Q = D_0\) with the label empty wall, and test the rule first on pictures whose right answer we can feel: the blends \(S_{\lambda} = (1 - \lambda) A_0 + \lambda D_0\) of A Plane of Cats: Linear Combinations of Images. No computer is needed, because both distances collapse by algebra. Since \(S_{\lambda} - A_0 = \lambda (D_0 - A_0)\) and \(S_{\lambda} - D_0 = (1 - \lambda)(A_0 - D_0)\text{,}\) the scaling property of the length gives
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{dist}(S_{\lambda}, A_0) = \lambda \, \| A_0 - D_0 \|, \qquad \operatorname{dist}(S_{\lambda}, D_0) = (1 - \lambda) \, \| A_0 - D_0 \|, \end{equation*}
    for \(0 \leq \lambda \leq 1\text{.}\) The rule therefore says cat for every \(\lambda \lt \frac{1}{2}\text{,}\) says empty wall for every \(\lambda \gt \frac{1}{2}\text{,}\) and falls exactly silent at \(\lambda = \frac{1}{2}\text{,}\) where the blend really is halfway to being either thing. Its first verdicts are the sensible ones.
    🔗
    Dragging the slider draws the blend and reports the two distances. Below \(\lambda = 0.5\) the cat corner is nearer and the verdict is cat; above it the verdict flips; at \(0.5\) the two distances print the same number and the rule declines, exactly as the algebra promised.
    🔗
    Blends are a tame test: they live on the line segment joining the two references, drawn in coordinates in 7.37(a). A real second photograph of either corner would not. Sensors add grain—each pixel arrives a little brighter or darker than the scene, by an amount that differs from shot to shot. Model it in the simplest way: each pixel receives its own random error, independent of the others, with mean \(0\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\) gray levels. A new photograph of the cat corner is then \(A_0 + G\text{,}\) where \(G\) is a picture of pure noise, and since gray levels below \(0\) or above \(255\) do not exist, the few pixels the grain pushes outside that range are clipped back to it. The cell below manufactures such photographs, with grain of a size you control, and scores the rule on two hundred of them.
    🔗
    At sigma = 25 the rule is nearly perfect, and that should be more surprising than it looks. Grain of that size moves a shot about \(25\sqrt{48} \approx 173\) units—farther than the \(125.2\) that separate the two corners in the first place.
     3 
    The number \(\sigma\sqrt{48}\) is the typical size of the displacement, not its exact value in every shot: each photograph lands a little nearer or a little farther, and only the average behaviour is pinned down by \(\sigma\text{.}\)
    A typical grainy photograph of the eye ends up farther from \(A_0\) than the empty corner is, and the rule still sorts them correctly. Why the verdicts survive a displacement larger than the gap between the classes is the central fact of the whole section, and it comes out of the next subsection in a single line.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.7.3 The Score and the Decision Boundary

    The rule as stated computes two distances and compares them. Expanding both with the identity (7.1) of The Distance Between Two Pictures shows that it is secretly doing something simpler. The picture \(X\) is closer to \(P\) than to \(Q\) exactly when \(\| X - P \|^2 \lt \| X - Q \|^2\text{,}\) that is, when
    \begin{equation*} \| X \|^2 - 2\, X \cdot P + \| P \|^2 \lt \| X \|^2 - 2\, X \cdot Q + \| Q \|^2. \end{equation*}
    The term \(\| X \|^2\) cancels from both sides—the length of the incoming picture never mattered at all—and what remains is \(2\, X \cdot (P - Q) \gt \| P \|^2 - \| Q \|^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Two pictures built from the references turn that inequality into geometry. Let
    \begin{equation*} M = \tfrac{1}{2}(P + Q) \qquad \text{and} \qquad W = P - Q \end{equation*}
    be the midpoint of the two references and the discriminating direction that runs from \(Q\) to \(P\text{.}\) Since \(\| P \|^2 - \| Q \|^2 = (P + Q) \cdot (P - Q) = 2\, M \cdot W\text{,}\) the inequality above reads \(X \cdot W \gt M \cdot W\text{,}\) or, with everything on one side,
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{dist}(X, P) \lt \operatorname{dist}(X, Q) \quad \text{if and only if} \quad (X - M) \cdot W \gt 0. \end{equation*}
    Define the score of a picture to be that quantity,
    \begin{equation*} s(X) = (X - M) \cdot W. \end{equation*}
    The rule says cat when the score is positive, empty wall when it is negative, and nothing when it is zero.
    🔗
    The score measures the incoming picture from the midpoint \(M\text{,}\) in the single direction \(W\) that points from the empty-wall reference toward the cat reference. Its sign says which side of the midpoint \(X\) lies on. The set where \(s(X) = 0\)—the pictures equidistant from both references, which the rule refuses to judge—is the flat through \(M\) perpendicular to \(W\text{,}\) the decision boundary that cuts picture space into a cat side and an empty side.
    🔗
    Multiplying out \((X - M) \cdot W\) puts the same score in the form a machine would store:
    \begin{equation*} s(X) = X \cdot W - \tau, \qquad \text{where} \quad \tau = M \cdot W = \tfrac{1}{2}\left( \| P \|^2 - \| Q \|^2 \right). \end{equation*}
    Everything the machine will ever decide is contained in one picture and one number: the weight picture \(W\)
     4 
    The name comes from the role the entries of \(W\) play in the sum. Written out, the score is \(X \cdot W - \tau = \sum_{i,j} x_{ij}\, w_{ij} - \tau\text{,}\) so each entry \(w_{ij}\) is the weight attached to pixel \((i,j)\text{:}\) how much that pixel’s brightness counts toward the verdict, and in which direction—positive entries vote cat, negative ones vote empty wall, and near-zero entries are ignored.
    and the threshold \(\tau\text{.}\) The two references themselves score
    \begin{equation*} s(P) = \tfrac{1}{2}\, \| W \|^2, \qquad s(Q) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\, \| W \|^2, \end{equation*}
    placed symmetrically on either side of the boundary, each at the same distance from it. They are not the ends of the scale, however: a picture farther out along \(W\) than \(P\) scores higher than \(P\) does, and scores lie anywhere on the number line. For our references \(P = A_0\) and \(Q = D_0\) the numbers are concrete. The squared lengths are \(\| A_0 \|^2 = 375632\) and \(\| D_0 \|^2 = 259930\text{,}\) so \(\tau = (375632 - 259930)/2 = 57851\text{,}\) and \(s(A_0) = +7839\) while \(s(D_0) = -7839\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The map \(T(X) = X \cdot W\) is a linear transformation from \(\mathbb{M}_{8,6}\) to \(\R\) in the sense of Definition 7.11, because the dot product is linear in its first argument; the score differs from it only by the constant \(\tau\text{.}\)
     5 
    Only \(T\) is a linear transformation. The score \(s(X) = T(X) - \tau\) is not one unless \(\tau = 0\text{,}\) since a linear transformation must send the zero picture to \(0\) and \(s(0) = -\tau\text{.}\) A linear transformation followed by the addition of a constant is called affine. The distinction costs nothing below, because every claim made about how the score changes is a claim about \(T\text{,}\) and the constant cancels out of differences.
    Linearity is not a spectator here—it is why the blends of the previous subsection behaved so cleanly: along the segment from \(A_0\) to \(D_0\) the score varies linearly with \(\lambda\text{,}\) \(s(S_\lambda) = 7839\,(1 - 2\lambda)\text{,}\) sliding at constant speed from \(+7839\) to \(-7839\) and crossing zero at exactly \(\lambda = \frac{1}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    As in The Matrix of a Linear Transformation, the transformation \(T\) has a matrix with respect to the pixel basis: the \(1 \times 48\) row that lists the \(48\) weights. Because \(W \neq 0\text{,}\) that row is not the zero row, so \(T\) has rank \(1\text{,}\) and Theorem 5.27 gives \(\dim \ker(T) = 48 - 1 = 47\text{.}\) The kernel is where the machine’s blindness lives. If \(G\) lies in it, then adding \(G\) to any picture leaves the verdict untouched:
    \begin{equation*} s(X + G) = s(X) + G \cdot W = s(X). \end{equation*}
    Note what this does and does not say. A picture \(G\) in \(\ker(T)\) need not have score zero itself; what is zero is the change it causes. So \(\ker(T)\) is the \(47\)-dimensional space of directions of change the classifier cannot detect, and recognition, for this machine, is one linear transformation followed by one comparison.
    🔗
    The weight picture itself is worth looking at, and it is an old friend: for these references, \(W = A_0 - D_0\) is exactly twice the difference picture \(\Delta\) of A Plane of Cats: Linear Combinations of Images. Its entries are near zero along the wall and the lighting that the two corners share, and largest in size at the eye, where they disagree (compare 7.39). The machine was never told where to look. Subtracting the two references built its attention for it: shared background cancels, and whatever remains is precisely what distinguishes the classes. The cell below draws \(W\text{,}\) shifted to mid-gray for display exactly as \(\Delta\) was, and confirms the scores.
    🔗
    The cell prints \(\tau = 57851\text{,}\) the symmetric scores \(\pm 7839\text{,}\) a score of exactly \(0\) at the midpoint, and a blend score matching the linear prediction. The drawing shows \(W\) against a mid-gray background standing for zero: featureless where the two corners agree, and bright and dark only where the eye is.
    🔗
    Read for an arbitrary \(G\) rather than one in the kernel, that same line settles the surprise left hanging by the grain. For any disturbance \(G\) whatever,
    \begin{equation*} s(X + G) = s(X) + G \cdot W, \end{equation*}
    so the verdict on \(X + G\) differs from the verdict on \(X\) only through the single number \(G \cdot W\)—never through the size of \(G\text{.}\) Grain is a picture whose \(48\) entries have no relationship to \(W\text{:}\) in the sum \(G \cdot W\) the positive and negative contributions arrive in comparable amounts and largely cancel, so grain can move a shot \(173\) units while barely touching the score. The distance saw an enormous displacement; the score, which is all that decides, saw almost none. A far shorter disturbance aimed squarely along \(W\) would do what the grain could not. 7.50 is the whole story in two dimensions.
    🔗

    Remark 7.51. One Neuron.

    An object that multiplies its inputs by fixed weights, adds, subtracts a threshold, and reports only the sign has a name: it is an artificial neuron, and a machine made of exactly one of them is the perceptron, proposed in 1958 as a model of how a nerve cell might decide.
     6 
    Frank Rosenblatt built the first one at the Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory; its intended application was, from the beginning, recognizing images.
    A modern image recognizer is millions of these composed in layers, with two upgrades: the weights are not obtained by averaging two references but adjusted step by step to reduce error on examples, and a simple nonlinear function is applied between layers—necessarily, since applying one linear transformation after another yields a transformation that is again linear, so that without the nonlinearity a whole stack of layers could do no more than one neuron pretending to be many. The scale is new; the arithmetic inside is the arithmetic of this section.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.7.4 Learning Prototypes from Many Photographs

    Everything so far has taken the two references for granted. No recognizer in practice is handed the true, grainless corner to use as one. It is handed photographs—several of each thing, every one contaminated—and must build its own references out of them. The tool for combining several pictures into one has been in hand since A Plane of Cats: Linear Combinations of Images: the average. Before proving anything about it, look at what it does. The cell below photographs the cat corner six times through deliberately brutal grain, sigma = 35, so that a single photograph is a poor memory, and puts the average of the six beside them.
    🔗
    The three photographs are a mess and no two are the same mess. Their average is visibly calmer, and the printed distances say so numerically: the average of six sits several times closer to the true corner than any single shot does. Grain pushes each pixel up or down at random, so at any given pixel the six errors largely cancel. A reference built this way is called a prototype. It is a picture no camera ever took—it was computed, not photographed.
    🔗
    Averaging is not merely one way of combining the photographs. In a precise sense it is the best one.
    🔗

    Theorem 7.52. The Average Is the Best Single Reference.

    Let \(X_1, \dots, X_k\) be pictures in \(\mathbb{M}_{mn}\) and let \(\overline{X} = \frac{1}{k}\left( X_1 + \cdots + X_k \right)\) be their average. Then for every picture \(C\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{i=1}^{k} \| X_i - C \|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \| X_i - \overline{X} \|^2 + k \, \| \overline{X} - C \|^2. \end{equation*}
    In particular, the total squared distance from the photographs to \(C\) is smallest for exactly one picture: \(C = \overline{X}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Write \(X_i - C = (X_i - \overline{X}) + (\overline{X} - C)\) and expand the square with the dot product:
    \begin{equation*} \| X_i - C \|^2 = \| X_i - \overline{X} \|^2 + 2\, (X_i - \overline{X}) \cdot (\overline{X} - C) + \| \overline{X} - C \|^2. \end{equation*}
    Sum over \(i\text{.}\) The middle terms contribute \(2 \left( \sum_{i=1}^{k} (X_i - \overline{X}) \right) \cdot (\overline{X} - C)\text{,}\) and the definition of the average says precisely that \(\sum_{i=1}^{k} (X_i - \overline{X}) = X_1 + \cdots + X_k - k\overline{X}\) is the zero picture. What survives is the claimed identity, and since \(k \| \overline{X} - C \|^2 \geq 0\) with equality only at \(C = \overline{X}\text{,}\) the minimum is at the average and nowhere else.
    🔗
    🔗
    The theorem settles one question and leaves another wide open.
     7 
    What it proves is that the average is the unique best single representative of the photographs in the least-squares sense: among all pictures, it and it alone makes the total of the squared distances to the photographs as small as possible. It does not prove that the classifier built from these prototypes has the highest classification accuracy obtainable from the same photographs. That is a different question, about verdicts rather than about distances, and this section does not answer it.
    It is also worth being plain about what the machine is given. The procedure is supervised: someone hands it photographs already labelled cat or empty wall, and it averages each labelled pile separately. It does not discover that there are two kinds of corner; it is told, and it compresses each told-about pile into one picture.
    🔗
    Nothing in The Score and the Decision Boundary used what \(P\) and \(Q\) were, so the classifier carries over untouched. Where it once ran on \(P = A_0\) and \(Q = D_0\text{,}\) it now runs on the two prototypes, with the weight picture \(W = P - Q\) and threshold \(\tau = M \cdot W\) rebuilt from them. The cell below compares the two machines head to head: the nearest-reference rule using a single grainy photograph as its memory of each corner, and the same rule using the prototypes.

    The single-photograph machine does markedly worse than the prototype machine, which recovers most of what the grain destroyed. This is the first moment in the book where a machine can fairly be said to learn: shown examples, it compresses them, by nothing deeper than a linear combination with coefficients \(\frac{1}{6}\text{,}\) into a reference it was never given.

    Remark 7.53. What the Average Is Good For.

    In this section the average earns its place three ways—one proved, one probabilistic, one demonstrated.
    1. It is the best one-picture summary of the pile. This is what Theorem 7.52 proves: if six labelled photographs must be compressed into a single reference, the average is the unique picture minimizing the total squared distance to them—any other choice \(C\) pays the extra \(k \| \overline{X} - C \|^2\text{.}\) Nothing about verdicts; it is purely a statement about faithful summarization.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. It is a denoiser—an estimate of the clean corner the machine was never given. Grain pushes each pixel up or down with mean zero, so at every pixel the \(k\) errors largely cancel, and the average of \(k\) shots lands on the order of \(\sqrt{k}\) times closer to the true corner than any single shot does—the improvement the first cell above prints. The prototype is the best reconstruction of \(A_0\) available from what the machine was actually handed.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Consequently it repairs the decision boundary, which is why the verdicts improve. The classifier depends on its references only through \(W = P - Q\) and \(\tau\text{:}\) noisy references mean a corrupted \(W\) and a misplaced boundary, while references closer to the true corners mean a boundary closer to the one built from \(A_0\) and \(D_0\) themselves. That is what the head-to-head comparison demonstrates.
      🔗
      🔗
    The footnote to Theorem 7.52 lives in the gap between the last two items: closest to the truth in least squares provably gives the best summary, and in the experiment gives better verdicts, but it does not prove that no other reference choice could classify the same photographs better still. The honest slogan is that the average is the best memory, not provably the best judge—and a better memory happens to make a better judge.
    🔗
    7.54 collects the whole story in one picture: how the machine decides, how its accuracy is measured, and what such a measurement does and does not establish.

    Subsection 7.7.5 Assessing the Accuracy of the Verdicts*

    This subsection is starred: it leans on a few ideas from probability that this book does not develop, and nothing later depends on it. It answers a question the previous subsection deliberately left open. Theorem 7.52 proves that the average is the best representative in the least-squares sense; to assess the classifier’s verdicts, we instead apply it to new labelled photographs that were not used to construct the prototypes. If it classifies \(c\) out of \(N\) such photographs correctly, then
    \begin{equation*} \text{test accuracy} = \frac{c}{N}. \end{equation*}
    This answers a different question from the theorem: not how faithfully the prototype summarizes its pile, but how often the resulting verdict is right.
    🔗
    For the estimate to be fair, four rules govern the experiment.
    1. Use labelled training photographs to form the cat and wall prototypes.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Create a separate collection of new labelled test photographs, none of which entered the prototypes.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Give exactly the same test photographs to every classifier being compared.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Count the correct verdicts and divide by the number tested.
      🔗
      🔗
    The head-to-head cell of the previous subsection quietly bent the third rule: it called accuracy twice, and each call manufactured its own random test set, so part of the printed difference is luck of the draw. The cell below repairs this. It builds one balanced test set and evaluates both classifiers on it. Two hundred trials are adequate for a demonstration; two thousand makes the estimate more stable.
    🔗
    The two printed numbers now differ only because the classifiers differ. Be precise about what they establish: the experiment estimates how accurately each particular classifier works on new photographs generated by the chosen noise model, and it can support the claim that the prototype classifier performs better in this experiment. It does not prove that no other possible classifier could do better. For real photographs the same principle applies—reserve labelled images that never enter the construction of the prototypes—and the test set should include the kinds of variation expected in practice, such as changes in exposure and position, not only synthetic grain.
    🔗

    An Exact Calculation for Gaussian Grain.

    The clean-reference classifier admits a calculation with no experiment in it at all. Assume the entries of the disturbance \(G\) are independent normal random variables with mean \(0\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\text{,}\) and temporarily ignore the clipping of gray levels at \(0\) and \(255\text{.}\) For a new cat photograph \(X = A_0 + G\text{,}\) linearity of the score gives
    \begin{equation*} s(A_0 + G) = \tfrac{1}{2}\| W \|^2 + G \cdot W, \end{equation*}
    and \(G \cdot W = \sum_{i,j} g_{ij} w_{ij}\text{,}\) a sum of independent normal terms, is itself normal with mean \(0\) and standard deviation \(\sigma \| W \|\text{.}\) The verdict is correct exactly when the score is positive, so
    \begin{equation*} P(\text{correct verdict}) = \Phi\!\left( \frac{\| W \|}{2\sigma} \right), \end{equation*}
    where \(\Phi\) is the cumulative distribution function of a standard normal random variable—the probability that such a variable falls below the given value. An empty-wall photograph gives the same probability, because the two classes sit symmetrically about the decision boundary.
    🔗
    For the values of this section, \(\| W \| = \sqrt{15678} \approx 125.2\) and \(\sigma = 25\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} \Phi(125.2 / 50) = \Phi(2.504) \approx 0.9939: \end{equation*}
    the expected accuracy is about \(99.4\) percent, which explains the nearly perfect two-hundred-photograph result of Recognition by Nearest Reference. Note also what the formula watches: only the ratio of the separation \(\| W \|\) to the grain \(\sigma\)—the total displacement \(\sigma\sqrt{48}\) never appears, which is the central fact of the section restated as a probability. The calculation is exact for the unclipped Gaussian model with clean references. Once the references are themselves noisy learned prototypes, the formula becomes less transparent, and the held-out test experiment above is the clearer choice.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.7.6 What the Method Does Not See

    Return to the clean references \(P = A_0\) and \(Q = D_0\text{,}\) so that the numbers are the concrete ones of The Score and the Decision Boundary. The line that saved the verdicts from grain,
    \begin{equation*} s(X + G) = s(X) + G \cdot W, \end{equation*}
    predicts exactly what will not be survived: a disturbance that pushes every pixel the same way. Underexposing a patch by \(t\) gray levels adds \(G = -tJ\text{,}\) where \(J\) is the all-ones picture, and \(J \cdot W\) is simply the sum of the entries of \(W\)—the total brightness of \(A_0\) minus the total brightness of \(D_0\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} J \cdot W = 4210 - 3522 = 688, \end{equation*}
    positive because the cat corner is on the whole brighter than the bare wall. Nothing cancels; all \(48\) terms push together. The score of a darkened eye patch is \(s(A_0) - 688\,t = 7839 - 688\,t\text{,}\) which turns negative as soon as \(t \gt \frac{7839}{688} \approx 11.4\text{.}\) Darken every pixel by twelve gray levels out of \(255\)—a displacement of only \(\| 12 J \| \approx 83\) units, less than half of what the grain inflicted—and the machine calls the cat’s eye an empty wall. Turning the exposure down by scaling does the same: \(s(cA_0) = 65690\,c - 57851\) crosses zero at \(c \approx 0.88\text{,}\) so any shot at less than \(88\) percent of the original exposure is pronounced catless. The machine that shrugged off grain which buried the picture is undone by a hint of dusk.
    🔗
    A second blindness costs nothing to state and everything in practice. Photograph the eye one pixel to the right, so that the identical content lands one column over, and the patch moves \(74\) units away from \(A_0\)—more than half the distance separating a cat from no cat at all. The verdict happens to survive this once, but the lesson does not improve with luck: the distance compares pixel against pixel by position, and has no way of knowing that two pictures contain the same thing in different places. Try all three failures below.
    🔗
    The printed table makes the point without the pictures: darkening moves the patch about \(83\) units and flips the verdict to empty wall; scaling moves it farther still and flips it too; the one-pixel shift moves it \(74\) units, more than either, and the verdict nevertheless holds at cat. Distance moved is not what decides. Only the score is.
    🔗
    Each failure names the repair it is waiting for, and each repair is a course that exists. Dividing out brightness so that only pattern remains, and measuring pictures along a few well-chosen directions instead of all \(48\) at once, are the business of orthogonality, projections, and least squares—the linear algebra that usually follows this book. And the deepest question raised here—the verdict lived or died with one direction, \(W\text{;}\) which directions in a space does a matrix itself single out as the ones that matter?—is not a later course at all. It is the next chapter.
    🔗
    This closes the story the cat has been carrying since Digital Images as Matrices. There, arithmetic acted on one picture; in A Plane of Cats: Linear Combinations of Images, two pictures spanned a plane; here, pictures are compared, averaged, and finally judged. A machine that tells a cat from a bare wall by squaring forty-eight subtractions is not yet seeing. But it is deciding, its decision is a linear transformation and a threshold, and the linear part of a modern image recognizer still consists largely of the operations studied here, run at scale through billions of weights instead of forty-eight: weighted sums, dot products, and linear transformations. What those recognizers have and this one does not is the nonlinear step of Remark 7.51, inserted between the linear stages. That step is where their additional power comes from, and it is not in this chapter.
