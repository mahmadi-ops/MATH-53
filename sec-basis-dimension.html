    Section 5.2 Basis and Dimension

    Subsection 5.2.1 Basis of a Subspace

    Definition 5.12. Basis.

    Let \(H\) be a subspace of \(\R^n\text{.}\) Then \(\{ \vec{u}_1, \ldots, \vec{u}_k \}\) is called a basis for \(H\) if the following conditions hold:
    🔗
    1. \(\operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{u}_1, \ldots, \vec{u}_k \} = H\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\{ \vec{u}_1, \ldots, \vec{u}_k \}\) is linearly independent.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.13. Two Different Bases for the Same Subspace.

    Consider the \(xy\)-plane, i.e., \(H = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} \in \R^3 \; \middle| \; z = 0 \right\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The most obvious basis is \(\mathcal{B}_1 = \{ \vec{e}_1, \vec{e}_2 \}\text{,}\) since \(H = \operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{e}_1, \vec{e}_2 \}\) and
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x} = x\vec{e}_1 + y\vec{e}_2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    But it is not the only one. Take \(\mathcal{B}_2 = \{ \vec{e}_1, \vec{e}_1 + \vec{e}_2 \}\text{.}\) Then \(H = \operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{e}_1, \vec{e}_1 + \vec{e}_2 \}\) and
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x} = (x - y)\vec{e}_1 + y \left( \vec{e}_1 + \vec{e}_2 \right). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A subspace has many bases — but, as we are about to see, they all have the same size.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.2.2 All Bases Have the Same Size

    Theorem 5.15. Spanning Sets Are at Least as Large as Independent Sets.

    Suppose \(\{ \vec{u}_1, \ldots, \vec{u}_r \}\) is a linearly independent set of vectors in \(\R^n\text{,}\) and each \(\vec{u}_k\) is contained in \(\operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{v}_1, \ldots, \vec{v}_s \}\text{.}\) Then \(s \geq r\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In words, spanning sets have at least as many vectors as linearly independent sets.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Since each \(\vec{u}_j\) is in \(\operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{v}_1, \ldots, \vec{v}_s \}\text{,}\) there exist scalars \(a_{ij}\) such that
    \begin{equation*} \vec{u}_j = \sum_{i=1}^{s} a_{ij} \vec{v}_i. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Suppose for a contradiction that \(s \lt r\text{.}\) Then the matrix \(A = \begin{bmatrix} a_{ij} \end{bmatrix}\) has fewer rows, \(s\text{,}\) than columns, \(r\text{.}\) Then the system \(AX = 0\) has a nontrivial solution \(\vec{d}\text{;}\) that is, there is a \(\vec{d} \neq \vec{0}\) such that \(A\vec{d} = \vec{0}\text{.}\) In other words,
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{j=1}^{r} a_{ij} d_j = 0, \qquad i = 1, 2, \ldots, s. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore
    \begin{align*} \sum_{j=1}^{r} d_j \vec{u}_j \amp = \sum_{j=1}^{r} d_j \sum_{i=1}^{s} a_{ij} \vec{v}_i\\ \amp = \sum_{i=1}^{s} \left( \sum_{j=1}^{r} a_{ij} d_j \right) \vec{v}_i = \sum_{i=1}^{s} 0 \vec{v}_i = \vec{0}, \end{align*}
    which contradicts the assumption that \(\{ \vec{u}_1, \ldots, \vec{u}_r \}\) is linearly independent, because not all the \(d_j\) are zero. Thus this contradiction indicates that \(s \geq r\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 5.16. Any Two Bases Have the Same Size.

    Let \(H\) be a subspace of \(\R^n\) and suppose \(\{ \vec{u}_1, \ldots, \vec{u}_r \}\) and \(\{ \vec{v}_1, \ldots, \vec{v}_s \}\) are two bases for \(H\text{.}\) Then \(r = s\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Note that any spanning set has at least as many vectors as a basis, by Theorem 5.15. Also, any basis is itself a spanning set. Hence:
    🔗
    1. \(\{ \vec{u}_1, \ldots, \vec{u}_r \}\) is a basis and \(\{ \vec{v}_1, \ldots, \vec{v}_s \}\) is a spanning set, therefore \(r \leq s\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\{ \vec{v}_1, \ldots, \vec{v}_s \}\) is a basis and \(\{ \vec{u}_1, \ldots, \vec{u}_r \}\) is a spanning set, therefore \(s \leq r\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Together these give \(r = s\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.2.3 Dimension

    Definition 5.17. Dimension.

    Let \(H\) be a subspace of \(\R^n\text{.}\) Then the dimension of \(H\) is the number of vectors in a basis of \(H\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 5.18.

    This definition makes sense only because of Theorem 5.16: different bases could otherwise give different answers.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.19. Computing Dimensions.

    What is the dimension of the following subspaces of \(\R^3\text{?}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H_1 = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} \in \R^3 \; \middle| \; x = y = z \right\}, \qquad H_2 = \left\{ \vec{u} \in \R^3 \; \middle| \; \vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = 0, \; \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(H_1\) is a line through the origin with the direction vector \(\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(H_1 = \operatorname{Span}\{ \vec{v} \}\text{,}\) hence
    \begin{equation*} \dim H_1 = 1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For \(H_2\text{,}\) write \(\vec{u} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Then \(\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = 2x - y + z = 0\text{,}\) which is a plane through the origin, and
    \begin{equation*} H_2 = \operatorname{Span}\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}. \end{equation*}
    Note that any two vectors on the plane that are linearly independent can be used here. Hence
    \begin{equation*} \dim H_2 = 2. \end{equation*}
