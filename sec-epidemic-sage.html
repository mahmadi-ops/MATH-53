    Section 9.3 Epidemic Dynamics

    In this section we model the spread of a disease through a population as a Markov chain, and we use the computer algebra system Sage to simulate the model and plot how the population evolves over time. In the process we will revisit the main themes of this chapter: transition matrices, stochastic matrices, steady states, and eigenvalues.
    🔗

    Subsection 9.3.1 The Model

    A disease is introduced into a population. In each period (say, days) we count the fraction of the population that is in four different infection states:
    • Susceptible. These individuals can acquire the disease the next day.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Infected. These individuals have the disease.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Recovered (and immune). These individuals had the disease and survived, and now have immunity.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Deceased. These individuals had the disease, and unfortunately died from it.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Let \(s_k\text{,}\) \(i_k\text{,}\) \(r_k\text{,}\) and \(d_k\) be the fractions of the population in each of the four states on day \(k\text{,}\) and collect them into the state vector
    \begin{equation*} \vec{S}_k = \begin{bmatrix} s_k \\ i_k \\ r_k \\ d_k \end{bmatrix} \in \R^4. \end{equation*}
    For example, \(\vec{S}_k = \begin{bmatrix} 0.75 \\ 0.10 \\ 0.10 \\ 0.05 \end{bmatrix}\) means that on day \(k\text{,}\) \(75\%\) of the population is susceptible, \(10\%\) is infected, \(10\%\) is recovered and immune, and \(5\%\) has died from the disease. Note that the entries of \(\vec{S}_k\) are nonnegative and add up to one, so \(\vec{S}_k\) is a probability vector.
    🔗
    Our model assumes the following happens over each day.
    • \(5\%\) of the susceptible population will acquire the disease. (The other \(95\%\) will remain susceptible.)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(1\%\) of the infected population will die from the disease, \(10\%\) will recover and acquire immunity, and \(4\%\) will recover and not acquire immunity (and therefore, become susceptible). The remaining \(85\%\) will remain infected.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Those who have recovered with immunity and those who have died remain in those states.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Let us translate these rules into equations. Tomorrow’s susceptible individuals are today’s susceptible individuals who did not become infected, which is \(0.95\,s_k\text{,}\) plus today’s infected individuals who recovered without immunity, which is \(0.04\,i_k\text{.}\) Tomorrow’s infected individuals are those who remain infected, \(0.85\,i_k\text{,}\) plus the susceptible individuals who acquire the disease, \(0.05\,s_k\text{.}\) Similar arguments for the last two states give the system
    \begin{align*} s_{k+1} \amp = 0.95\,s_k + 0.04\,i_k,\\ i_{k+1} \amp = 0.05\,s_k + 0.85\,i_k,\\ r_{k+1} \amp = r_k + 0.10\,i_k,\\ d_{k+1} \amp = d_k + 0.01\,i_k. \end{align*}
    We put these together in matrix form as \(\vec{S}_{k+1} = T\vec{S}_k\text{,}\) where the transition matrix is
    \begin{equation*} T = \begin{bmatrix} 0.95 \amp 0.04 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0.05 \amp 0.85 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0.10 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0.01 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Each column of \(T\) is a probability vector, so \(T\) is a stochastic matrix as in Definition 9.7. We will simulate the model starting from \(\vec{S}_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{:}\) everyone is susceptible, and the disease is about to be introduced. Iterating \(\vec{S}_{k+1} = T\vec{S}_k\) by hand for hundreds of days is hopeless, so we hand the iteration to Sage.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 9.3.2 Setting Up the Model in Sage

    Each block of code below is an interactive Sage cell: press Evaluate to run it in your browser. (You can also copy the code into the Sage cell server or a CoCalc worksheet.) Within a single page the cells share their variables, but they must be evaluated in order, so run each cell as you reach it.
    🔗
    Step 1: enter \(T\) and \(\vec{S}_0\text{.}\) In Sage, matrix(QQ, [...]) builds a matrix from a list of rows, and vector(QQ, [...]) builds a vector. The ring QQ tells Sage to store the entries as exact rational numbers, so no rounding errors accumulate during the iteration.
    🔗
    Step 2: check that \(T\) is stochastic. Before simulating, it is good practice to make sure we typed the matrix correctly. The command below computes the sum of the entries of each column of \(T\text{;}\) every sum should equal one.
    🔗
    Step 3: compute the first few days. Matrix-vector multiplication in Sage is written T*S, so one day of the epidemic is one multiplication. The loop below computes \(\vec{S}_1, \ldots, \vec{S}_5\text{;}\) the method .n(digits=4) displays each exact answer as a decimal with four significant digits.
    🔗
    Check the first line against the model: after one day we expect \(\vec{S}_1 = T\vec{S}_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 0.95 \\ 0.05 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) since on day zero there is nobody infected yet, and \(5\%\) of the susceptible population acquires the disease.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 9.3.3 Simulating and Plotting the Epidemic

    Step 4: simulate \(200\) days and plot the four fractions. To draw the graphs we store the whole history of the simulation: the list history holds \(\vec{S}_0, \vec{S}_1, \ldots, \vec{S}_{200}\text{,}\) where each new state is obtained by multiplying the previous one (history[-1] is the last entry of the list) by \(T\text{.}\) Then, for each state \(j = 1, 2, 3, 4\text{,}\) the command line plots the points \(\left( k, (\vec{S}_k)_j \right)\) for \(k = 0, 1, \ldots, 200\) and connects them with a curve. (In Sage, list and vector indices start at zero, so the entry \((\vec{S}_k)_j\) is written history[k][j-1]; the loop variable j below runs through \(0, 1, 2, 3\text{.}\)) Adding the four plots combines them into a single figure with a legend.
    🔗
    You should see the susceptible fraction decay from one towards zero, the infected fraction rise to a peak of roughly \(21\%\) around day \(11\) and then die out, and the recovered and deceased fractions climb towards limiting values. The simulation shows that after around \(100\) days, the state converges to one with a little under \(10\%\) of the population deceased, and the remaining population immune.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 9.3.4 The Long Run

    Step 5: where does the epidemic end? The plot suggests that the state vectors converge. To see the limit numerically, ask Sage for a state far in the future, say \(\vec{S}_{1000} = T^{1000}\,\vec{S}_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    So in the long run about \(90.9\%\) of the population ends up recovered and immune, and about \(9.1\%\) deceased. (In Exercises 9.3.5 you will show that the exact limits are \(\frac{10}{11}\) and \(\frac{1}{11}\text{.}\))
    🔗
    Step 6: compare with the eigenvalue point of view. By Example 9.10, the stochastic matrix \(T\) must have eigenvalue one. Let us ask Sage for all the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of \(T\text{.}\) The command below returns a list of triples (eigenvalue, [eigenvectors], multiplicity).
    🔗
    Sage reports the eigenvalue \(\lambda = 1\) with multiplicity two, together with the two independent eigenvectors
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \vec{x}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    and two further eigenvalues with absolute value less than one. This is consistent with what the simulation showed: the states “everyone recovered” and “everyone deceased” are both fixed by \(T\text{,}\) and so is any probability vector \(a\vec{x}_1 + b\vec{x}_2\) built from them. The epidemic therefore has infinitely many steady states, and which one the population converges to depends on the initial state \(\vec{S}_0\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Remark 9.18. Why the Steady State Is Not Unique.

    Does this contradict Theorem 9.12? No: that theorem assumes the transition matrix is regular, and \(T\) is not. However high the power \(T^k\text{,}\) its third and fourth columns remain \(\vec{x}_1\) and \(\vec{x}_2\) (recovered and deceased individuals never leave their states), so \(T^k\) always has zero entries. You can confirm this in Sage by evaluating, say, T^50.
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises 9.3.5 Exercises

    1.

    Rerun the simulation of Subsection 9.3.3 with the initial state \(\vec{S}_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 0.75 \\ 0.10 \\ 0.10 \\ 0.05 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Do the state vectors still converge? Do they converge to the same limit as before? Explain how your answer is consistent with Remark 9.18.

    2.

    Suppose a vaccine becomes available, and each day \(2\%\) of the susceptible population is vaccinated, acquiring immunity without ever being infected (so vaccinated individuals move directly from susceptible to recovered). Modify the equations of the model, write down the new transition matrix, and adapt your Sage simulation. How does the graph change, and what fraction of the population is deceased in the long run?
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Show that with \(\vec{S}_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) the deceased fraction converges to exactly \(\frac{1}{11}\text{,}\) and the recovered fraction to exactly \(\frac{10}{11}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Each day, \(0.01\,i_k\) is added to the deceased fraction and \(0.10\,i_k\) to the recovered fraction, so their limits are \(0.01\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} i_k\) and \(0.10\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} i_k\) in the ratio \(1 : 10\text{.}\) Since eventually everyone is either recovered or deceased, the two limits add up to one.
