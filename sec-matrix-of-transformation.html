    Section 7.5 The Matrix of a Linear Transformation

    A linear transformation has no single matrix: it has one matrix per choice of basis for the domain and codomain. Changing those choices conjugates the matrix by a change of basis matrix, and a well-chosen basis can make the matrix strikingly simple.
    🔗

    Subsection 7.5.1 Four Matrices for One Transformation

    Example 7.26. The Same Transformation in Different Bases.

    Let \(T : \R^2 \to \R^2\) be the linear transformation given by
    \begin{equation*} T \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -5x + 6y \\ -3x + 4y \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    and let \(\mathcal{B} = \{ \vec{e}_1, \vec{e}_2 \} = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}\) and \(\mathcal{B}' = \{ \vec{b}_1, \vec{b}_2 \} = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}\) be the bases.
    🔗
    1. Compute the matrix \(A_1\) of \(T\) with respect to \(\mathcal{B}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Compute the matrix \(A_2\) of \(T\) with respect to \(\mathcal{B}'\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Find the matrix \(A_3\) of \(T\) if the bases \(\mathcal{B}\) and \(\mathcal{B}'\) are used for domain and codomain respectively.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Find the matrix \(A_4\) of \(T\) if the bases \(\mathcal{B}'\) and \(\mathcal{B}\) are used for domain and codomain respectively.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    First note the relations between the two bases, which we will need throughout:
    \begin{equation*} \vec{e}_1 = -\vec{b}_1 + \vec{b}_2, \qquad \vec{e}_2 = 2\vec{b}_1 - \vec{b}_2, \end{equation*}
    so that
    \begin{equation*} P_{\mathcal{B}' \leftarrow \mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \amp 2 \\ 1 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad P_{\mathcal{B} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}'} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (I) Since \(T \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -5 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(T \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} A_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -5 \amp 6 \\ -3 \amp 4 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (II) We compute the images of the new basis vectors and express them in the new basis:
    \begin{equation*} T \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad \left[ \begin{matrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{matrix} \right]_{\mathcal{B}'} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} T \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -4 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad \left[ \begin{matrix} -4 \\ -2 \end{matrix} \right]_{\mathcal{B}'} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    which gives
    \begin{equation*} A_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Alternatively, starting from \(A_1 [\vec{u}]_{\mathcal{B}} = [\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}}\) and inserting \(P^{-1}P = I\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} P_{\mathcal{B}' \leftarrow \mathcal{B}} \, A_1 \, \underbrace{P_{\mathcal{B}' \leftarrow \mathcal{B}}^{-1} P_{\mathcal{B}' \leftarrow \mathcal{B}}}_{I} [\vec{u}]_{\mathcal{B}} = P_{\mathcal{B}' \leftarrow \mathcal{B}} [\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}}, \end{equation*}
    i.e., \(A_2 [\vec{u}]_{\mathcal{B}'} = [\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}'}\) where
    \begin{equation*} A_2 = P_{\mathcal{B}' \leftarrow \mathcal{B}} \, A_1 \, P_{\mathcal{B}' \leftarrow \mathcal{B}}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \amp 2 \\ 1 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -5 \amp 6 \\ -3 \amp 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -2 \end{bmatrix}. \checkmark \end{equation*}
    Note how much simpler \(A_2\) is than \(A_1\text{:}\) in the basis \(\mathcal{B}'\) the transformation just scales \(\vec{b}_1\) by \(1\) and \(\vec{b}_2\) by \(-2\text{.}\) Indeed
    \begin{equation*} T \left( x\vec{b}_1 + y\vec{b}_2 \right) = x \, T(\vec{b}_1) + y \, T(\vec{b}_2) = x\vec{b}_1 - 2y\vec{b}_2. \end{equation*}
    This is exactly the phenomenon that Chapter 8 is about.
    🔗
    (III) First method. Express the images of the \(\mathcal{B}\) basis vectors in the \(\mathcal{B}'\) basis:
    \begin{equation*} \left[ T(\vec{e}_1) \right]_{\mathcal{B}'} = \left[ \begin{matrix} -5 \\ -3 \end{matrix} \right]_{\mathcal{B}'} = -1 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - 2 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \; \longrightarrow \; \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \left[ T(\vec{e}_2) \right]_{\mathcal{B}'} = \left[ \begin{matrix} 6 \\ 4 \end{matrix} \right]_{\mathcal{B}'} = 2 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + 2 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \; \longrightarrow \; \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    so \(A_3 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \amp 2 \\ -2 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (III) Second method. From \(A_1 [\vec{u}]_{\mathcal{B}} = [\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}}\text{,}\) multiply both sides by \(P_{\mathcal{B}' \leftarrow \mathcal{B}}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} A_3 = P_{\mathcal{B}' \leftarrow \mathcal{B}} \, A_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \amp 2 \\ 1 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -5 \amp 6 \\ -3 \amp 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \amp 2 \\ -2 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}. \checkmark \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (IV) First method.
    \begin{equation*} \left[ T(\vec{b}_1) \right]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \left[ T(\vec{b}_2) \right]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} -4 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad A_4 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -4 \\ 1 \amp -2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (IV) Second method.
    \begin{equation*} A_4 = A_1 \, P_{\mathcal{B} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}'} = \begin{bmatrix} -5 \amp 6 \\ -3 \amp 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -4 \\ 1 \amp -2 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    or equally
    \begin{equation*} A_4 = P_{\mathcal{B} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}'} \, A_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -4 \\ 1 \amp -2 \end{bmatrix}. \checkmark \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 7.27. The Same Game in \(\mathbb{P}_2\).

    Let \(T : \mathbb{P}_2 \to \mathbb{P}_2\) be the linear transformation
    \begin{equation*} T\left( p(x) \right) = p(x) + p'(x), \end{equation*}
    and consider the ordered bases \(\mathcal{B}_1 = \{ 1, x, x^2 \}\) and \(\mathcal{B}_2 = \{ 1, \; 1 + x, \; 1 + x + x^2 \}\) of \(\mathbb{P}_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Compute the matrix \(A_1\) of \(T\) with respect to \(\mathcal{B}_1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Compute the matrix \(A_2\) of \(T\) with respect to \(\mathcal{B}_2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Find the matrix \(A_3\) of \(T\) if the bases \(\mathcal{B}_1\) and \(\mathcal{B}_2\) are used for domain and codomain respectively.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Find the matrix \(A_4\) of \(T\) if the bases \(\mathcal{B}_2\) and \(\mathcal{B}_1\) are used for domain and codomain respectively.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Unlike the previous example, the vectors here are polynomials, not columns. The coordinate maps are the bridge: they are isomorphisms \(\mathbb{P}_2 \to \R^3\text{,}\) and once every polynomial is replaced by its coordinate vector, all four computations become the same matrix arithmetic in \(\R^3\) as before.
    🔗
    First the change-of-basis matrices, exactly as in Example 7.32: reading the elements of \(\mathcal{B}_2\) in the basis \(\mathcal{B}_1\) columnwise, and inverting,
    \begin{equation*} P_{\mathcal{B}_1 \leftarrow \mathcal{B}_2} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad P_{\mathcal{B}_2 \leftarrow \mathcal{B}_1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (I) Transform the basis elements of \(\mathcal{B}_1\) and read the outputs in \(\mathcal{B}_1\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} T(1) = 1, \qquad T(x) = x + 1, \qquad T(x^2) = x^2 + 2x, \end{equation*}
    whose coordinate vectors form the columns
    \begin{equation*} A_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (II) First method. Transform the basis elements of \(\mathcal{B}_2\) and express the outputs in \(\mathcal{B}_2\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} T(1) \amp = 1 = 1(1) \; \longrightarrow \; (1, 0, 0),\\ T(1 + x) \amp = 2 + x = 1(1) + 1(1 + x) \; \longrightarrow \; (1, 1, 0),\\ T(1 + x + x^2) \amp = 2 + 3x + x^2 = -1(1) + 2(1 + x) + 1(1 + x + x^2) \; \longrightarrow \; (-1, 2, 1), \end{align*}
    so
    \begin{equation*} A_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (II) Second method. Exactly as in the previous example,
    \begin{equation*} A_2 = P_{\mathcal{B}_2 \leftarrow \mathcal{B}_1} \, A_1 \, P_{\mathcal{B}_1 \leftarrow \mathcal{B}_2} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \checkmark \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (III) Multiplying \(A_1 [\, p \,]_{\mathcal{B}_1} = [\, T(p) \,]_{\mathcal{B}_1}\) on the left by \(P_{\mathcal{B}_2 \leftarrow \mathcal{B}_1}\) converts the output coordinates,
    \begin{equation*} A_3 = P_{\mathcal{B}_2 \leftarrow \mathcal{B}_1} \, A_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp -2 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    As a spot check on the second column: \(T(x) = 1 + x\) is itself the second element of \(\mathcal{B}_2\text{,}\) so its \(\mathcal{B}_2\)-coordinates are \((0, 1, 0)\text{.}\) \(\checkmark\)
    🔗
    (IV) This time the input coordinates arrive in \(\mathcal{B}_2\) and must be converted before \(A_1\) can act:
    \begin{equation*} A_4 = A_1 \, P_{\mathcal{B}_1 \leftarrow \mathcal{B}_2} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 2 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    As a spot check on the third column: \(T(1 + x + x^2) = 2 + 3x + x^2\text{,}\) whose \(\mathcal{B}_1\)-coordinates are \((2, 3, 1)\text{.}\) \(\checkmark\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.5.2 A Transformation between Different Vector Spaces

    Example 7.28. From \(\mathbb{P}_2\) to \(\mathbb{M}_{22}\).

    Let \(\mathbb{M}_{22}\) be the space of all \(2 \times 2\) matrices and let \(\mathbb{P}_2\) be the space of all polynomials of at most degree \(2\text{.}\) Define \(S : \mathbb{P}_2 \to \mathbb{M}_{22}\) as the transformation given by
    \begin{equation*} S \left( a_2 x^2 + a_1 x + a_0 \right) = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 + a_2 \amp a_2 + a_0 \\ a_1 - a_0 \amp a_1 + a_0 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    for all \(a_2 x^2 + a_1 x + a_0 \in \mathbb{P}_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Describe the null space of \(S\text{.}\) Is \(S\) one-to-one?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Express the range of \(S\) as a span of a linearly independent set of \(2 \times 2\) matrices. Is \(S\) onto?
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Is \(S\) an isomorphism?
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Extend the basis of the range of \(S\) such that the extended basis, \(\mathcal{B}_1\text{,}\) spans \(\mathbb{M}_{22}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Consider the ordered basis \(\mathcal{B} = \{ 1, x, x^2 \}\) and the ordered basis \(\mathcal{B}_1\) for \(\mathbb{M}_{22}\) and find the matrix of the transformation \(S\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    6. Consider the ordered basis \(\mathcal{B} = \{ 1, x, x^2 \}\) and the ordered basis
      \begin{equation*} \mathcal{B}_2 = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \right\} \end{equation*}
      for \(\mathbb{M}_{22}\) and find the matrix of the transformation \(S\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (1)
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Nul} S = \left\{ P(x) = a_2 x^2 + a_1 x + a_0 \; : \; S(P(x)) = 0_{2 \times 2} \right\}. \end{equation*}
    Setting \(S(P(x)) = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 + a_2 \amp a_2 + a_0 \\ a_1 - a_0 \amp a_1 + a_0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\) gives
    \begin{equation*} a_1 + a_2 = 0, \quad a_2 + a_0 = 0, \quad a_1 - a_0 = 0, \quad a_1 + a_0 = 0, \end{equation*}
    and the last two force \(a_1 = a_0 = 0\text{,}\) whence \(a_2 = 0\text{.}\) This is the trivial solution.
    🔗
    Hence the null space only contains the zero vector of \(\mathbb{P}_2\text{,}\) which is known as the zero polynomial \(P_0(x) = (0)x^2 + 0(x) + 0(1) = 0\text{,}\) and \(S\) is one-to-one:
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Nul} S = \{ P_0(x) \}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (2) The output of \(S\) is
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} a_1 + a_2 \amp a_2 + a_0 \\ a_1 - a_0 \amp a_1 + a_0 \end{bmatrix} = a_0 \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{M_0} + a_1 \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{M_1} + a_2 \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{M_2}. \end{equation*}
    We can check that \(\{ M_0, M_1, M_2 \}\) is a linearly independent set and hence this set is a basis for the range of \(S\text{,}\) since the range of \(S\) is \(\operatorname{Span}\{ M_0, M_1, M_2 \}\text{.}\) This means \(\dim (\text{range of } S) = 3\text{,}\) whereas \(\dim (\mathbb{M}_{22}) = 4\text{.}\) Therefore \(S\) is NOT onto.
    🔗
    (3) No. \(S\) is not onto.
    🔗
    (4) We want to add a fourth matrix, \(M_3\text{,}\) such that \(M_3\) cannot be written as \(M_3 = a_0 M_0 + a_1 M_1 + a_2 M_2\text{.}\) In other words, writing \(M_3 = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \amp b_2 \\ b_3 \amp b_4 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) the following must NOT have a solution:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \amp b_2 \\ b_3 \amp b_4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 + a_2 \amp a_2 + a_0 \\ a_1 - a_0 \amp a_1 + a_0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    As a system of linear equations in \(a_2, a_1, a_0\) we have
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \amp b_1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp b_2 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -1 \amp b_3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \amp b_4 \end{array} \right] \longrightarrow \cdots \longrightarrow \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \amp b_1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \amp b_4 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -1 \amp b_3 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp b_3 + b_2 - b_1 \end{array} \right]. \end{equation*}
    If \(b_3 + b_2 - b_1 \neq 0\text{,}\) then the system is inconsistent. For example, choose \(b_1 = 1\text{,}\) \(b_2 = b_3 = b_4 = 0\text{,}\) which gives \(M_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Therefore, the set
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{B}_1 = \{ M_0, M_1, M_2, M_3 \} = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \right\} \end{equation*}
    is a basis for \(\mathbb{M}_{22}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (5) The strategy is captured by the following diagram. The coordinate maps identify \(\mathbb{P}_2\) with \(\R^3\) and \(\mathbb{M}_{22}\) with \(\R^4\text{,}\) so the transformation \(S\) corresponds to a matrix transformation \(\R^3 \to \R^4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    At the level of the ordered bases, \(S\) sends the basis \(\{1, x, x^2\}\) of \(\mathbb{P}_2\) to the four matrices \(\{M_0, M_1, M_2, M_3\}\) spanning \(\mathbb{M}_{22}\text{,}\) and the induced matrix transformation is given by a matrix \(A\text{:}\)
    🔗
    Here is how the elements of \(\mathcal{B}\) are transformed:
    \begin{equation*} S(1) = M_0, \qquad S(x) = M_1, \qquad S(x^2) = M_2, \end{equation*}
    which means, reading each image in the basis \(\mathcal{B}_1\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} A_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}_{4 \times 3}. \end{equation*}
    To find \(A_1\) we had to express \(S(1)\text{,}\) \(S(x)\text{,}\) \(S(x^2)\) in terms of the basis elements \(M_0, M_1, M_2\text{,}\) and \(M_3\text{.}\) In other words, we needed to figure out how the basis elements of the domain transform with respect to the basis \(\mathcal{B}_1\) of the codomain.
    🔗
    After finding the output of the matrix transformation, we can use the isomorphism between \(\mathbb{M}_{22}\) and \(\R^4\) and write the output as a \(2 \times 2\) matrix.
    🔗
    (6) Write \(M_{12} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(M_{21} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(M_{22} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) and \(M_{11} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) so that \(\mathcal{B}_2 = \{ M_{12}, M_{21}, M_{22}, M_{11} \}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{align*} S(1) \amp = M_0 = M_{12} - M_{21} + M_{22},\\ S(x) \amp = M_1 = M_{21} + M_{22} + M_{11},\\ S(x^2) \amp = M_2 = M_{12} + M_{11}, \end{align*}
    and therefore
    \begin{equation*} A_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Alternatively, we can find a matrix that changes the basis \(\mathcal{B}_1\) to \(\mathcal{B}_2\text{.}\) From
    \begin{equation*} M_0 = M_{12} - M_{21} + M_{22}, \quad M_1 = M_{21} + M_{22} + M_{11}, \quad M_2 = M_{12} + M_{11}, \quad M_3 = M_{11}, \end{equation*}
    we get
    \begin{equation*} P_{\mathcal{B}_2 \leftarrow \mathcal{B}_1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ -1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \text{and} \qquad P_{\mathcal{B}_2 \leftarrow \mathcal{B}_1} \, A_1 = A_2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The reason is the familiar one: from \(A_1 [\vec{u}]_{\mathcal{B}} = [\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}_1}\text{,}\) multiplying both sides by \(P_{\mathcal{B}_2 \leftarrow \mathcal{B}_1}\) gives \(A_2 [\vec{u}]_{\mathcal{B}} = [\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}_2}\text{.}\)

    Example 7.31. The Trace as a Transformation.

    The trace of a square matrix is the sum of its diagonal entries. On \(\mathbb{M}_{22}\) it defines the map \(\operatorname{tr} : \mathbb{M}_{22} \to \R\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{tr} \begin{bmatrix} a \amp b \\ c \amp d \end{bmatrix} = a + d, \end{equation*}
    where the codomain \(\R\) is itself a vector space, of dimension \(1\text{,}\) with ordered basis \(\{ 1 \}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Show that \(\operatorname{tr}\) is a linear transformation.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Describe \(\operatorname{Nul} (\operatorname{tr})\) and find a basis for it. Is \(\operatorname{tr}\) one-to-one?
      🔗
      🔗
    3. What is the range of \(\operatorname{tr}\text{?}\) Is it onto?
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Is \(\operatorname{tr}\) an isomorphism?
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Verify the rank–nullity relation for \(\operatorname{tr}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    6. Consider the ordered basis \(\mathcal{B} = \{ M_{11}, M_{12}, M_{21}, M_{22} \}\) of \(\mathbb{M}_{22}\text{,}\) where \(M_{ij}\) has a \(1\) in position \((i,j)\) and zeros elsewhere, and the ordered basis \(\{1\}\) of \(\R\text{.}\) Find the matrix of the transformation \(\operatorname{tr}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    7. Use that matrix to compute \(\operatorname{tr} \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp -3 \\ 7 \amp -9 \end{bmatrix}\) through coordinates.
      🔗
      🔗
    8. What is the matrix of \(\operatorname{tr}\) if the basis of the codomain is changed to \(\{ 2 \}\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (1) For matrices \(M = \begin{bmatrix} a \amp b \\ c \amp d \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(N = \begin{bmatrix} a' \amp b' \\ c' \amp d' \end{bmatrix}\) and a scalar \(k\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} \operatorname{tr}(M + N) \amp = (a + a') + (d + d') = \operatorname{tr} M + \operatorname{tr} N,\\ \operatorname{tr}(k M) \amp = ka + kd = k \operatorname{tr} M, \end{align*}
    so \(\operatorname{tr}\) respects addition and scalar multiplication and is a linear transformation.
    🔗
    (2) A matrix is in the null space exactly when its diagonal entries cancel:
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Nul} (\operatorname{tr}) = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} a \amp b \\ c \amp -a \end{bmatrix} \; : \; a, b, c \in \R \right\} = \operatorname{Span} \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \right\}. \end{equation*}
    The three spanning matrices are linearly independent (they occupy different entries), so they form a basis and \(\dim \operatorname{Nul} (\operatorname{tr}) = 3\text{.}\) Since the null space contains nonzero vectors — for instance \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix} \mapsto 0\) — the transformation is NOT one-to-one.
    🔗
    (3) Every real number is a trace: for any \(r \in \R\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{tr} \begin{bmatrix} r \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} = r. \end{equation*}
    Hence the range is all of \(\R\) and \(\operatorname{tr}\) is onto.
    🔗
    (4) No. An isomorphism must be one-to-one and onto, and by (2) the trace is not one-to-one. (No transformation \(\mathbb{M}_{22} \to \R\) could be an isomorphism: the dimensions \(4\) and \(1\) do not match.)
    🔗
    (5) From (2) and (3),
    \begin{equation*} \underbrace{\dim (\text{range of } \operatorname{tr})}_{1} + \underbrace{\dim \operatorname{Nul} (\operatorname{tr})}_{3} = 4 = \dim (\mathbb{M}_{22}), \end{equation*}
    as the rank–nullity relation predicts.
    🔗
    (6) We transform the basis elements of the domain and read each output in the basis \(\{1\}\) of \(\R\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{tr}(M_{11}) = 1, \quad \operatorname{tr}(M_{12}) = 0, \quad \operatorname{tr}(M_{21}) = 0, \quad \operatorname{tr}(M_{22}) = 1. \end{equation*}
    Each column of the matrix is the coordinate vector of one output, which here is a single number, so the matrix of the transformation is the \(1 \times 4\) row
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The corresponding matrix transformation \(\R^4 \to \R\) tells the same story as (2) and (3) in the language of pivots: \(A\) has a pivot in its only row, so it is onto, but it cannot have a pivot in every one of its four columns, so it is not one-to-one.
    🔗
    (7) In the basis \(\mathcal{B}\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp -3 \\ 7 \amp -9 \end{bmatrix} \right]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ -3 \\ 7 \\ -9 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ -3 \\ 7 \\ -9 \end{bmatrix} = 4 - 9 = -5, \end{equation*}
    which agrees with computing the trace directly: \(4 + (-9) = -5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (8) With respect to the basis \(\{2\}\) of \(\R\text{,}\) a real number \(r\) has coordinate \(r/2\text{,}\) since \(r = \tfrac{r}{2}(2)\text{.}\) Each entry of the matrix is now the \(\{2\}\)-coordinate of the corresponding output:
    \begin{equation*} A' = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac{1}{2} \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp \tfrac{1}{2} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    As in the previous example, changing a basis of the codomain rescales the rows of the matrix without changing the transformation itself.

    Example 7.32. A Change of Basis in \(\mathbb{P}_2\).

    Consider the standard ordered basis \(\mathcal{B}_1 = \{ 1, x, x^2 \}\) of \(\mathbb{P}_2\) and the set
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{B}_2 = \{ 1, \; 1 + x, \; 1 + x + x^2 \}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    1. Show that \(\mathcal{B}_2\) is a basis of \(\mathbb{P}_2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the change-of-basis matrix \(P_{\mathcal{B}_2 \leftarrow \mathcal{B}_1}\text{,}\) which converts \(\mathcal{B}_1\)-coordinates into \(\mathcal{B}_2\)-coordinates.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Find the coordinates of \(q(x) = 3 + 2x + 4x^2\) relative to the basis \(\mathcal{B}_2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (1) Since \(\dim \mathbb{P}_2 = 3\text{,}\) it is enough to show that the three polynomials are linearly independent. Suppose
    \begin{equation*} a(1) + b(1 + x) + c(1 + x + x^2) = 0, \end{equation*}
    that is,
    \begin{equation*} (a + b + c) + (b + c)x + c x^2 = 0. \end{equation*}
    Comparing coefficients gives \(c = 0\text{,}\) then \(b + c = 0\) forces \(b = 0\text{,}\) and then \(a + b + c = 0\) forces \(a = 0\text{.}\) Only the trivial combination produces the zero polynomial, so \(\mathcal{B}_2\) is linearly independent and hence a basis of \(\mathbb{P}_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (2) The easy direction is the other one: each element of \(\mathcal{B}_2\) is already written in terms of \(\{1, x, x^2\}\text{,}\) so reading off coordinates columnwise,
    \begin{equation*} P_{\mathcal{B}_1 \leftarrow \mathcal{B}_2} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The matrix we want is its inverse, \(P_{\mathcal{B}_2 \leftarrow \mathcal{B}_1} = \left( P_{\mathcal{B}_1 \leftarrow \mathcal{B}_2} \right)^{-1}\text{,}\) which we can also compute directly by expressing each element of \(\mathcal{B}_1\) in the basis \(\mathcal{B}_2\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} 1 = (1), \qquad x = (1 + x) - (1), \qquad x^2 = (1 + x + x^2) - (1 + x), \end{equation*}
    so that
    \begin{equation*} P_{\mathcal{B}_2 \leftarrow \mathcal{B}_1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    In the language of this section, \(P_{\mathcal{B}_2 \leftarrow \mathcal{B}_1}\) is the matrix of the identity transformation \(\operatorname{id} : \mathbb{P}_2 \to \mathbb{P}_2\) with respect to the basis \(\mathcal{B}_1\) of the domain and the basis \(\mathcal{B}_2\) of the codomain: the transformation does nothing, and the matrix only translates between the two coordinate systems.
    🔗
    (3) Relative to \(\mathcal{B}_1\) the coordinates of \(q\) are read off directly, and the matrix converts them:
    \begin{equation*} [q(x)]_{\mathcal{B}_1} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad [q(x)]_{\mathcal{B}_2} = P_{\mathcal{B}_2 \leftarrow \mathcal{B}_1} [q(x)]_{\mathcal{B}_1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    As a check,
    \begin{equation*} 1(1) - 2(1 + x) + 4(1 + x + x^2) = (1 - 2 + 4) + (-2 + 4)x + 4x^2 = 3 + 2x + 4x^2 = q(x). \end{equation*}
