    Problems for Markov Chains and Steady States (Section 9.1).
    🔗

    1. Steady-State Vectors.

    Find a steady-state vector for the Markov chain \(S_{n+1} = PS_n\) with transition matrix
    \begin{equation*} \text{(a)} \quad P = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{3} \amp \frac{1}{4} \\[2pt] \frac{2}{3} \amp \frac{3}{4} \end{bmatrix} \qquad \text{(b)} \quad P = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{2} \amp \frac{1}{3} \\[2pt] \frac{1}{2} \amp \frac{2}{3} \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Both matrices are stochastic, since every column sums to \(1\text{,}\) so by Theorem 9.13 a steady state is a solution of \((P - I)\vec{x} = \vec{0}\) whose entries sum to \(1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a)
    \begin{equation*} P - I = \begin{bmatrix} -\frac{2}{3} \amp \frac{1}{4} \\[2pt] \frac{2}{3} \amp -\frac{1}{4} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The two rows are negatives of each other, so the single equation is \(-\tfrac{2}{3}x_1 + \tfrac{1}{4}x_2 = 0\text{,}\) i.e. \(x_2 = \tfrac{8}{3}x_1\text{.}\) Taking \(x_1 = 3\) gives \((3, 8)\text{,}\) which sums to \(11\text{,}\) so the steady state is
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{3}{11} \\[2pt] \frac{8}{11} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (b)
    \begin{equation*} P - I = \begin{bmatrix} -\frac{1}{2} \amp \frac{1}{3} \\[2pt] \frac{1}{2} \amp -\frac{1}{3} \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    giving \(x_2 = \tfrac{3}{2}x_1\text{.}\) Taking \(x_1 = 2\) gives \((2,3)\text{,}\) which sums to \(5\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{2}{5} \\[2pt] \frac{3}{5} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Both \(P\) have all entries positive, hence are regular (Definition 9.4), so by Theorem 9.12 these steady states are unique and every starting distribution converges to them.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. ★ Bob’s Coffee.

    Bob always drinks his morning coffee either at café \(X\) or at café \(Y\text{.}\) Suppose that
    • on day \(0\) he is certainly at \(X\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • he never goes to \(Y\) two days in a row;
      🔗
      🔗
    • if he is at \(X\) one day, the next day he is twice as likely to be at \(Y\) as at \(X\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Write the transition matrix and compute the state vectors for the first few days. What do you notice?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The initial state vector is \(\vec{S}_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) The second condition says that from \(Y\) he always moves to \(X\text{;}\) the third says that from \(X\) he stays with probability \(\tfrac{1}{3}\) and moves with probability \(\tfrac{2}{3}\text{.}\) Indexing columns by today and rows by tomorrow,
    \begin{equation*} T = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{3} \amp 1 \\[2pt] \frac{2}{3} \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Each column sums to \(1\text{,}\) so \(T\) is stochastic.
    🔗
    Iterating \(\vec{S}_{k+1} = T\vec{S}_k\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} \vec{S}_1 \amp = \begin{bmatrix} 0.3333 \\ 0.6667 \end{bmatrix},\\ \vec{S}_2 \amp = \begin{bmatrix} 0.7778 \\ 0.2222 \end{bmatrix},\\ \vec{S}_3 \amp = \begin{bmatrix} 0.4815 \\ 0.5185 \end{bmatrix},\\ \vec{S}_4 \amp = \begin{bmatrix} 0.6790 \\ 0.3210 \end{bmatrix},\\ \vec{S}_8 \amp = \begin{bmatrix} 0.6031 \\ 0.3969 \end{bmatrix}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    The state vectors oscillate but close in on \(\begin{bmatrix} 0.6 \\ 0.4 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Trying other starting vectors gives the same limit, so the long-run behaviour appears not to depend on where Bob started. That limit is computed exactly in Exercise 10.9.8.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. ★ A Regular Matrix.

    Show that \(T = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{3} \amp 1 \\[2pt] \frac{2}{3} \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\) is regular.
    🔗
    Solution.
    By Definition 9.4, we need some power of \(T\) with all entries positive. The matrix itself has a zero in the \((2,2)\) position, so try the square:
    \begin{equation*} T^2 = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{3} \amp 1 \\[2pt] \frac{2}{3} \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{3} \amp 1 \\[2pt] \frac{2}{3} \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{7}{9} \amp \frac{1}{3} \\[2pt] \frac{2}{9} \amp \frac{2}{3} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Every entry of \(T^2\) is positive, so \(T\) is regular.
    🔗
    In words: even though Bob cannot go from \(Y\) to \(Y\) in one day, he can in two (via \(X\)), and that is exactly what the positive \((2,2)\)-entry of \(T^2\) records.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. ★ A Matrix That Is Not Regular.

    Show that \(P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp \frac{1}{2} \\[2pt] 0 \amp \frac{1}{2} \end{bmatrix}\) is stochastic but not regular.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Both columns sum to \(1\text{,}\) so \(P\) is stochastic. Computing powers,
    \begin{equation*} P^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp \frac{3}{4} \\[2pt] 0 \amp \frac{1}{4} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad P^3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp \frac{7}{8} \\[2pt] 0 \amp \frac{1}{8} \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    and in general
    \begin{equation*} P^n = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 - 2^{-n} \\[2pt] 0 \amp 2^{-n} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The \((2,1)\)-entry is \(0\) for every \(n\text{,}\) so no power of \(P\) has all entries positive and \(P\) is not regular.
    🔗
    The reason is structural: state \(1\) is absorbing. Once the chain reaches it, the first column of \(P\) keeps it there forever, so state \(2\) can never be reached from state \(1\text{,}\) no matter how many steps are allowed.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. ★ Stochastic Matrices Preserve Probability Vectors.

    A probability vector is a vector with non-negative entries summing to \(1\text{.}\) Show that if \(T\) is stochastic and \(\vec{x}\) is a probability vector, then so is \(T\vec{x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Non-negativity. The \(i\)th entry of \(T\vec{x}\) is
    \begin{equation*} \left( T\vec{x} \right)_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n} T_{ij} x_j, \end{equation*}
    a sum of products of non-negative numbers, since every \(T_{ij} \geq 0\) and every \(x_j \geq 0\text{.}\) So every entry of \(T\vec{x}\) is non-negative.
    🔗
    The entries still sum to one. By Example 9.9, a stochastic matrix preserves the sum of entries. Since the entries of \(\vec{x}\) sum to \(1\text{,}\) so do those of \(T\vec{x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hence \(T\vec{x}\) is again a probability vector. This is what makes the whole model coherent: a state vector of a Markov chain remains a legitimate list of probabilities at every step, however many transitions are applied.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6. ★ Powers of a Stochastic Matrix Are Stochastic.

    Show that if \(T\) is stochastic, then \(T^k\) is stochastic for every integer \(k \geq 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Write \(\vec{e} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ \vdots \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Saying that every column of \(T\) sums to \(1\) is exactly saying that
    \begin{equation*} \vec{e}^{\,T} T = \vec{e}^{\,T}, \end{equation*}
    since the \(j\)th entry of \(\vec{e}^{\,T}T\) is the sum of column \(j\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now argue by induction. The case \(k = 1\) is the hypothesis. If \(\vec{e}^{\,T}T^{k} = \vec{e}^{\,T}\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} \vec{e}^{\,T} T^{k+1} = \left( \vec{e}^{\,T} T^{k} \right) T = \vec{e}^{\,T} T = \vec{e}^{\,T}, \end{equation*}
    so the columns of \(T^{k+1}\) also sum to \(1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The entries of \(T^k\) are non-negative too, being sums of products of non-negative numbers. So \(T^k\) is stochastic.
    🔗
    This is what licenses the phrase “\(k\) steps of the chain”: applying \(T\) repeatedly is the same as applying the single stochastic matrix \(T^k\text{,}\) and the result is still a Markov transition.
    🔗
    🔗

    7. ★ Checking the Eigenvalue Bound.

    Find both eigenvalues of \(T = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{3} \amp 1 \\[2pt] \frac{2}{3} \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\) and confirm that they satisfy \(|\lambda| \leq 1\text{,}\) with \(\lambda = 1\) among them.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The characteristic polynomial is
    \begin{align*} c_T(\lambda) \amp = \begin{vmatrix} \lambda - \frac{1}{3} \amp -1 \\[2pt] -\frac{2}{3} \amp \lambda \end{vmatrix}\\ \amp = \lambda^2 - \tfrac{1}{3}\lambda - \tfrac{2}{3}\\ \amp = (\lambda - 1)\left( \lambda + \tfrac{2}{3} \right). \end{align*}
    So the eigenvalues are \(\lambda = 1\) and \(\lambda = -\tfrac{2}{3}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Both bounds hold: \(|1| = 1\) and \(\left| -\tfrac{2}{3} \right| = \tfrac{2}{3} \lt 1\text{,}\) as Example 9.11 guarantees for any stochastic matrix, and \(\lambda = 1\) appears as Example 9.10 promises.
    🔗
    The gap between the two matters. The second eigenvalue has absolute value strictly less than \(1\text{,}\) so its contribution dies away under repeated application, leaving only the \(\lambda = 1\) direction — which is why the state vectors in Exercise 10.9.2 settled down.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    8. ★ The Steady State for Bob’s Coffee.

    Does \(T = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{3} \amp 1 \\[2pt] \frac{2}{3} \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\) have a unique steady state vector? If so, find it.
    🔗
    Solution.
    (I) \(T\) is stochastic, since each column sums to \(1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (II) \(T\) is regular, by Exercise 10.9.3.
    🔗
    From (I) and (II) and Theorem 9.12, \(T\) has a unique steady state vector.
    🔗
    To find it, solve \((T - I)\vec{S} = \vec{0}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{cc|c} -\frac{2}{3} \amp 1 \amp 0 \\[2pt] \frac{2}{3} \amp -1 \amp 0 \end{array} \right] \longrightarrow \left[ \begin{array}{cc|c} 2 \amp -3 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{array} \right], \end{equation*}
    so \(2a = 3b\) and \(\vec{S} = t\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The entries are probabilities, so they must sum to \(1\text{:}\) \(5t = 1\) gives \(t = \tfrac{1}{5}\) and
    \begin{equation*} \vec{S} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{3}{5} \\[2pt] \frac{2}{5} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 \\ 0.4 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    confirming the numerical limit observed in Exercise 10.9.2. Checking, \(T(0.6, 0.4) = (0.2 + 0.4,\ 0.4) = (0.6, 0.4)\text{.}\) \(\checkmark\)
    🔗
    🔗

    9. ★ Why the Steady State Is Reached.

    For \(T = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{3} \amp 1 \\[2pt] \frac{2}{3} \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) diagonalize \(T\) and use the result to show that \(T^n \vec{x}_0\) converges to the steady state for every probability vector \(\vec{x}_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The eigenvalues are \(1\) and \(-\tfrac{2}{3}\text{,}\) by Exercise 10.9.7. For \(\lambda = 1\) the eigenvector is \((3,2)\text{,}\) from Exercise 10.9.8. For \(\lambda = -\tfrac{2}{3}\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} T + \tfrac{2}{3}I = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\[2pt] \frac{2}{3} \amp \frac{2}{3} \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    gives \(a + b = 0\text{,}\) so \((1,-1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hence \(T = PDP^{-1}\) with
    \begin{equation*} P = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad D = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\[2pt] 0 \amp -\frac{2}{3} \end{bmatrix}, \quad P^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp -3 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Then \(T^n = PD^nP^{-1}\text{,}\) and since \(\left( -\tfrac{2}{3} \right)^n \to 0\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} D^n \longrightarrow \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad T^n \longrightarrow \tfrac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp 3 \\ 2 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Finally, apply this limit to any probability vector \(\vec{x}_0 = (a,b)\) with \(a + b = 1\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \tfrac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp 3 \\ 2 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix} = \tfrac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 3(a+b) \\ 2(a+b) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{3}{5} \\[2pt] \frac{2}{5} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The starting vector has vanished from the answer, which is precisely the claim. The mechanism is the eigenvalue gap: everything except the \(\lambda = 1\) direction decays.
    🔗

    10. A Migration Matrix and a Population.

    Consider the migration matrix
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 \amp 0 \amp 0.1 \\ 0.2 \amp 0.8 \amp 0 \\ 0.2 \amp 0.2 \amp 0.9 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    for locations \(1\text{,}\) \(2\) and \(3\text{.}\) Initially there are \(100\) residents in location \(1\text{,}\) \(200\) in location \(2\) and \(400\) in location \(3\text{.}\) Find the population of each location in the long run.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Each column of \(A\) sums to \(1\text{,}\) so \(A\) is stochastic (Definition 9.7) and has \(1\) as an eigenvalue. Solve \((A - I)\vec{x} = \vec{0}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} A - I = \begin{bmatrix} -0.4 \amp 0 \amp 0.1 \\ 0.2 \amp -0.2 \amp 0 \\ 0.2 \amp 0.2 \amp -0.1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Row \(1\) gives \(x_3 = 4x_1\) and row \(2\) gives \(x_2 = x_1\text{;}\) row \(3\) is then automatically satisfied, since \(0.2 + 0.2 - 0.4 = 0\text{.}\) So the eigenvectors for \(\lambda = 1\) are the multiples of
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Migration moves people around but does not create or destroy them, so the total population stays at \(100 + 200 + 400 = 700\) forever. The entries of \([1\ 1\ 4]^T\) sum to \(6\text{,}\) so rescale by \(\tfrac{700}{6}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{700}{6}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{350}{3} \\[2pt] \frac{350}{3} \\[2pt] \frac{1400}{3} \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 116.7 \\ 116.7 \\ 466.7 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    So in the long run locations \(1\) and \(2\) each hold \(\tfrac{350}{3}\) residents and location \(3\) holds \(\tfrac{1400}{3}\text{.}\) As a check, these sum to \(\tfrac{2100}{3} = 700\text{.}\) \(\checkmark\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    11. Long-Run Proportions.

    A migration matrix is given by
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{5} \amp \frac{1}{2} \amp \frac{1}{5} \\[2pt] \frac{1}{4} \amp \frac{1}{4} \amp \frac{1}{2} \\[2pt] \frac{11}{20} \amp \frac{1}{4} \amp \frac{3}{10} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Compare the populations of the three locations in the long run.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Every column sums to \(1\text{:}\) for instance the first is \(\tfrac{4}{20} + \tfrac{5}{20} + \tfrac{11}{20} = 1\text{.}\) So \(A\) is stochastic and \(\lambda = 1\) is an eigenvalue.
    🔗
    Set \(x_3 = 1\) and solve \((A - I)\vec{x} = \vec{0}\text{.}\) The first two rows read
    \begin{align*} -\tfrac{4}{5}x_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}x_2 + \tfrac{1}{5} \amp = 0\\ \tfrac{1}{4}x_1 - \tfrac{3}{4}x_2 + \tfrac{1}{2} \amp = 0 . \end{align*}
    Clearing denominators gives \(8x_1 - 5x_2 = 2\) and \(x_1 - 3x_2 = -2\text{.}\) Substituting \(x_1 = 3x_2 - 2\) into the first,
    \begin{equation*} 8(3x_2 - 2) - 5x_2 = 2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 19x_2 = 18, \end{equation*}
    so \(x_2 = \tfrac{18}{19}\) and \(x_1 = \tfrac{54}{19} - \tfrac{38}{19} = \tfrac{16}{19}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The third row is then satisfied automatically, as it must be: \(\tfrac{11}{20}\cdot\tfrac{16}{19} + \tfrac{1}{4}\cdot\tfrac{18}{19} - \tfrac{7}{10} = 0\text{.}\) \(\checkmark\)
    🔗
    So the steady-state direction is \(\left[ \tfrac{16}{19}\ \tfrac{18}{19}\ 1 \right]^T\text{,}\) whose entries sum to \(\tfrac{53}{19}\text{.}\) Rescaling to make the entries proportions,
    \begin{equation*} \frac{19}{53} \begin{bmatrix} \frac{16}{19} \\[2pt] \frac{18}{19} \\[2pt] 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{16}{53} \\[2pt] \frac{18}{53} \\[2pt] \frac{19}{53} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    In the long run \(\tfrac{16}{53}\) of the population lives in location \(1\text{,}\) \(\tfrac{18}{53}\) in location \(2\text{,}\) and \(\tfrac{19}{53}\) in location \(3\) — whatever the starting distribution, since all entries of \(A\) are positive and so \(A\) is regular.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    12. ★ A Bike-Share Network.

    A bike-share company has three stations. Let \(A\) be the matrix whose \((i,j)\)-entry is the probability that a bike rented at station \(j\) is returned to station \(i\text{,}\) and suppose
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.4 \amp 0.2 \amp 0.2 \\ 0.2 \amp 0.4 \amp 0.2 \\ 0.4 \amp 0.4 \amp 0.6 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Initially the bikes are distributed as \(\vec{V}_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 20 \\ 30 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) How are they distributed in the long run?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Each column sums to \(1\text{,}\) so \(A\) is stochastic and has \(1\) as an eigenvalue. All entries are positive, so \(A\) is regular and the steady state is unique.
    🔗
    Solve \((A - I)\vec{x} = \vec{0}\text{.}\) Multiplying through by \(10\) to clear decimals,
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} -6 \amp 2 \amp 2 \\ 2 \amp -6 \amp 2 \\ 4 \amp 4 \amp -4 \end{bmatrix} \longrightarrow \begin{bmatrix} -3 \amp 1 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp -3 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    the last row being the negative of the sum of the first two, as it must be for a stochastic matrix.
    🔗
    The two remaining equations are \(3x = y + z\) and \(3y = x + z\text{.}\) Subtracting gives \(3(x - y) = y - x\text{,}\) so \(4(x-y) = 0\) and \(x = y\text{.}\) Then \(3x = x + z\) gives \(z = 2x\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x} = t \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Bikes are neither created nor destroyed, so the total stays at \(10 + 20 + 30 = 60\text{.}\) The entries of \((1,1,2)\) sum to \(4\text{,}\) so take \(t = 15\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \vec{V}_\infty = \begin{bmatrix} 15 \\ 15 \\ 30 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    that is \(25\%\text{,}\) \(25\%\) and \(50\%\) of the fleet. Checking, \(A(15,15,30) = (6+3+6,\ 3+6+6,\ 6+6+18) = (15,15,30)\text{.}\) \(\checkmark\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    13. ★ A Deer Population.

    A deer population consists of \(40\) fawns and \(40\) adult does. Suppose that
    1. the number of fawns born in any year is twice the number of adult does in the previous year;
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(70\%\) of adult does survive to the next year;
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(30\%\) of fawns survive to become adult does.
      🔗
      🔗
    Will the population survive?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(\vec{V}_k = \begin{bmatrix} f_k \\ a_k \end{bmatrix}\) record fawns and adults in year \(k\text{.}\) The three rules say
    \begin{equation*} f_{k+1} = 2a_k, \qquad a_{k+1} = 0.3 f_k + 0.7 a_k, \end{equation*}
    so \(\vec{V}_{k+1} = A\vec{V}_k\) with
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 2 \\ 0.3 \amp 0.7 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Note \(A\) is not stochastic: the population is not conserved.
    🔗
    The long-run behaviour is governed by the dominant eigenvalue. The characteristic polynomial is
    \begin{equation*} c_A(\lambda) = \lambda(\lambda - 0.7) - 0.6 = \lambda^2 - 0.7\lambda - 0.6, \end{equation*}
    whose roots are
    \begin{equation*} \lambda = \frac{0.7 \pm \sqrt{0.49 + 2.4}}{2} = \frac{0.7 \pm 1.7}{2}, \end{equation*}
    that is \(\lambda_1 = 1.2\) and \(\lambda_2 = -0.5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The dominant eigenvalue is \(1.2 \gt 1\text{,}\) so the population survives and in fact grows by about \(20\%\) per year.
    🔗
    For the eigenvector of \(\lambda_1 = 1.2\text{,}\) \(A - 1.2I = \begin{bmatrix} -1.2 \amp 2 \\ 0.3 \amp -0.5 \end{bmatrix}\) gives \(1.2f = 2a\text{,}\) so \((f,a) = (5,3)\text{.}\) Checking, \(A(5,3) = (6,\ 1.5 + 2.1) = (6, 3.6) = 1.2(5,3)\text{.}\) \(\checkmark\)
    🔗
    So whatever the starting counts, the herd settles into the ratio \(5\) fawns to \(3\) adult does, and thereafter multiplies by \(1.2\) each year. The second eigenvalue \(-0.5\) has absolute value less than \(1\text{,}\) so its contribution dies out; had the dominant eigenvalue been less than \(1\text{,}\) the herd would have dwindled to nothing instead.
    🔗

    Problems for The PageRank Algorithm: The 25 Billion Dollar Eigenvector (Section 9.4).
    🔗
    These problems rebuild the development of Section 9.4 on a small web of three pages, with links
    \begin{equation*} 1 \to 2, \quad 1 \to 3, \quad 2 \to 3, \quad 3 \to 1. \end{equation*}
    So page \(1\) links out to pages \(2\) and \(3\text{,}\) page \(2\) links only to page \(3\text{,}\) and page \(3\) links only to page \(1\text{.}\)
    🔗

    14. ★ Question 1.

    A surfer starts by choosing one of the \(N = 3\) pages at random. Let \(p_i(t)\) be the probability that the surfer is on page \(i\) at time \(t\text{.}\) What are \(p_i(0)\) and \(p_i(t+1)\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    At time \(0\text{.}\) All three pages are equally likely, so
    \begin{equation*} p_i(0) = \frac{1}{N} = \frac{1}{3} \qquad \text{for } i = 1, 2, 3. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    At time \(t+1\text{.}\) The surfer arrives at page \(i\) by having been on some page \(j\) that links to \(i\text{,}\) and then choosing the link to \(i\) from among the \(\ell_j\) outbound links of page \(j\text{.}\) So
    \begin{equation*} p_i(t+1) = \sum_{j \to i} \frac{p_j(t)}{\ell_j}, \end{equation*}
    where the sum runs over the pages \(j\) that link to \(i\) and \(\ell_j\) is the number of outbound links of page \(j\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Here \(\ell_1 = 2\text{,}\) \(\ell_2 = 1\text{,}\) \(\ell_3 = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    15. ★ Question 2.

    Find \(p_3(t+1)\) for this web.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Which pages link to page \(3\text{?}\) Both page \(1\) and page \(2\) do. Page \(1\) has \(\ell_1 = 2\) outbound links, so a surfer there picks the link to page \(3\) with probability \(\tfrac{1}{2}\text{.}\) Page \(2\) has \(\ell_2 = 1\text{,}\) so a surfer there goes to page \(3\) with certainty. Therefore
    \begin{equation*} p_3(t+1) = \frac{p_1(t)}{2} + p_2(t). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For comparison, page \(1\) is reached only from page \(3\text{,}\) giving \(p_1(t+1) = p_3(t)\text{,}\) and page \(2\) only from page \(1\text{,}\) giving \(p_2(t+1) = \tfrac{1}{2}p_1(t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    16. ★ Question 3.

    Find the matrix \(L\) representing the evolution of the probabilities from time \(t\) to time \(t+1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Collect the three relations of Exercise 10.9.15 into \(\vec{p}(t+1) = L\vec{p}(t)\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} L = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \\ \frac{1}{2} \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ \frac{1}{2} \amp 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Column \(j\) records where a surfer on page \(j\) goes: column \(1\) splits \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) and \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) between pages \(2\) and \(3\text{;}\) columns \(2\) and \(3\) each send everything to a single page.
    🔗
    Every column sums to \(1\text{,}\) so \(L\) is stochastic (Definition 9.7) — as it must be, since the surfer certainly lands somewhere.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    17. ★ Question 4.

    Suppose a fourth page is added with no outbound links, and no page links to it. Modify the matrix, assuming that a surfer on a page with no outbound link jumps to one of the \(4\) pages at random.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Page \(4\) is a dangling node: \(\ell_4 = 0\text{,}\) so the recipe \(p_j(t)/\ell_j\) makes no sense and its column of the link matrix would be all zeros. Such a matrix is not stochastic, and probability would leak away.
    🔗
    The fix is to declare that a surfer stuck on page \(4\) picks any of the \(N = 4\) pages uniformly, which fills that column with \(\tfrac{1}{4}\)s:
    \begin{equation*} L = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp \frac{1}{4} \\ \frac{1}{2} \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp \frac{1}{4} \\ \frac{1}{2} \amp 1 \amp 0 \amp \frac{1}{4} \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp \frac{1}{4} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Every column again sums to \(1\text{,}\) so \(L\) is stochastic. Note that the fourth row is nearly empty: nobody links to page \(4\text{,}\) so it is reached only by the random jump out of itself, and its long-run share will be very small.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    18. ★ Question 5.

    Let \(\alpha\) be the probability that the surfer stays interested and follows a link, the damping factor. How are the probabilities \(p_i(t+1)\) modified?
    🔗
    Solution.
    With probability \(\alpha\) the surfer follows a link, exactly as before; with probability \(1 - \alpha\) the surfer gets bored and jumps to a page chosen uniformly at random from all \(N\) pages, which contributes \(\dfrac{1 - \alpha}{N}\) to every page. So
    \begin{equation*} p_i(t+1) = \alpha \sum_{j \to i} \frac{p_j(t)}{\ell_j} + \frac{1 - \alpha}{N}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The two terms are the two things a surfer can do, weighted by how likely each is. Setting \(\alpha = 1\) recovers Exercise 10.9.14.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    19. ★ Question 6.

    Express the previous answer as a matrix equation, and show that the resulting matrix is stochastic and regular.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(J\) be the \(N \times N\) matrix all of whose entries are \(1\text{.}\) Since the entries of \(\vec{p}(t)\) sum to \(1\text{,}\) we have \(J\vec{p}(t) = \vec{e}\text{,}\) the all-ones vector, so the constant term \(\frac{1-\alpha}{N}\) in every coordinate can be written as \(\frac{1-\alpha}{N}J\vec{p}(t)\text{.}\) Hence
    \begin{equation*} \vec{p}(t+1) = M \vec{p}(t), \qquad M = \alpha L + \frac{1 - \alpha}{N} J. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \(M\) is stochastic. Each column of \(L\) sums to \(1\) and each column of \(J\) sums to \(N\text{,}\) so each column of \(M\) sums to
    \begin{equation*} \alpha (1) + \frac{1 - \alpha}{N}(N) = \alpha + 1 - \alpha = 1. \end{equation*}
    Its entries are non-negative, being non-negative combinations of non-negative numbers.
    🔗
    \(M\) is regular. For \(0 \lt \alpha \lt 1\) every entry of \(\frac{1-\alpha}{N}J\) is strictly positive, and adding the non-negative matrix \(\alpha L\) cannot destroy that. So \(M\) itself already has all entries positive, and \(M^1\) witnesses regularity.
    🔗
    By Theorem 9.12, \(M\) therefore has a unique steady state, and every starting distribution converges to it. That steady state is the PageRank vector — and the damping factor is what guarantees it exists.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    20. ★ Question 7.

    Take \(\alpha = \tfrac{1}{2}\) and find the steady state of the Markov chain for the three-page web, using the matrix \(L\) of Exercise 10.9.16. Which page ranks highest?
    🔗
    Solution.
    With \(N = 3\) and \(\alpha = \tfrac{1}{2}\text{,}\) the damping term contributes \(\frac{1-\alpha}{N} = \frac{1}{6}\) to every entry, so
    \begin{equation*} M = \tfrac{1}{2}L + \tfrac{1}{6}J = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{6} \amp \frac{1}{6} \amp \frac{2}{3} \\[2pt] \frac{5}{12} \amp \frac{1}{6} \amp \frac{1}{6} \\[2pt] \frac{5}{12} \amp \frac{2}{3} \amp \frac{1}{6} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Each column sums to \(1\text{.}\) \(\checkmark\)
    🔗
    Solve \((M - I)\vec{x} = \vec{0}\text{.}\) Clearing denominators, the first two rows give
    \begin{equation*} -5x_1 + x_2 + 4x_3 = 0, \qquad 5x_1 - 10x_2 + 2x_3 = 0. \end{equation*}
    Adding them gives \(-9x_2 + 6x_3 = 0\text{,}\) so \(x_3 = \tfrac{3}{2}x_2\text{.}\) Substituting into the first, \(-5x_1 + x_2 + 6x_2 = 0\text{,}\) so \(x_1 = \tfrac{7}{5}x_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Taking \(x_2 = 10\) gives \(x_1 = 14\) and \(x_3 = 15\text{,}\) which sum to \(39\text{.}\) Normalizing,
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{14}{39} \\[2pt] \frac{10}{39} \\[2pt] \frac{15}{39} \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 0.359 \\ 0.256 \\ 0.385 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The third row of \(M - I\) is satisfied automatically, as it must be: \(5(14) + 8(10) - 10(15) = 0\text{.}\) \(\checkmark\)
    🔗
    Page \(3\) ranks highest, then page \(1\text{,}\) then page \(2\text{.}\) That is reasonable: page \(3\) is the only page with two incoming links, and one of them comes from page \(1\text{,}\) which is itself well ranked. PageRank rewards not just being linked to, but being linked to by pages that are themselves important.

    21. ★ Two Other Ways to Let Go.

    For the coupled carts of Section 9.5, with normal modes \(\vec{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\) at frequency \(\omega_1\) and \(\vec{x}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\) at frequency \(\omega_2\text{,}\) find \(\vec{u}(t)\) when both carts are released from rest with
    \begin{equation*} \text{(a) } \vec{u}(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \text{(b) } \vec{u}(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    In both parts the carts start at rest, so as in Example 9.32 every sine coefficient vanishes and
    \begin{equation*} \vec{u}(t) = a_1 \cos(\omega_1 t)\vec{x}_1 + a_2 \cos(\omega_2 t)\vec{x}_2, \end{equation*}
    where \(a_1, a_2\) are the coordinates of \(\vec{u}(0)\) in the eigenvector basis.
    🔗
    (a) Here \(\vec{u}(0) = \vec{x}_1\) exactly, so \(a_1 = 1\) and \(a_2 = 0\text{,}\) and
    \begin{equation*} \vec{u}(t) = \cos(\omega_1 t) \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Both carts move together, in step, forever: a pure slow mode. The middle spring is never stretched or compressed, so no energy is ever traded, and the motion is exactly periodic with period \(2\pi/\omega_1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) Now solve
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = a_1 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + a_2 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    giving \(a_1 + a_2 = 0\) and \(a_1 - a_2 = 1\text{,}\) so \(a_1 = \tfrac{1}{2}\) and \(a_2 = -\tfrac{1}{2}\text{.}\) Hence
    \begin{align*} u_1(t) \amp = \tfrac{1}{2}\cos(\omega_1 t) - \tfrac{1}{2}\cos(\omega_2 t),\\ u_2(t) \amp = \tfrac{1}{2}\cos(\omega_1 t) + \tfrac{1}{2}\cos(\omega_2 t). \end{align*}
    🔗
    This is the mirror image of the example: pulling the second cart gives the same messy energy-trading motion with the roles of the carts exchanged. Since \(\omega_2 / \omega_1 = \sqrt{3}\) is irrational, the two cosines never come back into step and the motion never exactly repeats.
    🔗
    The contrast between (a) and (b) is the whole point of diagonalizing: released along an eigenvector the system is simple, and released along anything else it is a superposition of the two simple motions.
