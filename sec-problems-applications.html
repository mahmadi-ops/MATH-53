    Problems for Markov Chains and Steady States (Section 9.1).
    🔗

    1. Steady-State Vectors.

    Find a steady-state vector for the Markov chain \(S_{n+1} = PS_n\) with transition matrix
    \begin{equation*} \text{(a)} \quad P = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{3} \amp \frac{1}{4} \\[2pt] \frac{2}{3} \amp \frac{3}{4} \end{bmatrix} \qquad \text{(b)} \quad P = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{2} \amp \frac{1}{3} \\[2pt] \frac{1}{2} \amp \frac{2}{3} \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    2. ★ Bob’s Coffee.

    Bob always drinks his morning coffee either at café \(X\) or at café \(Y\text{.}\) Suppose that
    • on day \(0\) he is certainly at \(X\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • he never goes to \(Y\) two days in a row;
      🔗
      🔗
    • if he is at \(X\) one day, the next day he is twice as likely to be at \(Y\) as at \(X\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Write the transition matrix and compute the state vectors for the first few days. What do you notice?
    🔗
    🔗

    3. ★ A Regular Matrix.

    Show that \(T = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{3} \amp 1 \\[2pt] \frac{2}{3} \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\) is regular.
    🔗
    🔗

    4. ★ A Matrix That Is Not Regular.

    Show that \(P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp \frac{1}{2} \\[2pt] 0 \amp \frac{1}{2} \end{bmatrix}\) is stochastic but not regular.
    🔗
    🔗

    5. ★ Stochastic Matrices Preserve Probability Vectors.

    A probability vector is a vector with non-negative entries summing to \(1\text{.}\) Show that if \(T\) is stochastic and \(\vec{x}\) is a probability vector, then so is \(T\vec{x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    6. ★ Powers of a Stochastic Matrix Are Stochastic.

    Show that if \(T\) is stochastic, then \(T^k\) is stochastic for every integer \(k \geq 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    7. ★ Checking the Eigenvalue Bound.

    Find both eigenvalues of \(T = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{3} \amp 1 \\[2pt] \frac{2}{3} \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\) and confirm that they satisfy \(|\lambda| \leq 1\text{,}\) with \(\lambda = 1\) among them.
    🔗
    🔗

    8. ★ The Steady State for Bob’s Coffee.

    Does \(T = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{3} \amp 1 \\[2pt] \frac{2}{3} \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\) have a unique steady state vector? If so, find it.
    🔗
    🔗

    9. ★ Why the Steady State Is Reached.

    For \(T = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{3} \amp 1 \\[2pt] \frac{2}{3} \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) diagonalize \(T\) and use the result to show that \(T^n \vec{x}_0\) converges to the steady state for every probability vector \(\vec{x}_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Problems for Long-Run Behaviour: Two More Examples (Section 9.2).
    🔗

    10. A Migration Matrix and a Population.

    Consider the migration matrix
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 \amp 0 \amp 0.1 \\ 0.2 \amp 0.8 \amp 0 \\ 0.2 \amp 0.2 \amp 0.9 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    for locations \(1\text{,}\) \(2\) and \(3\text{.}\) Initially there are \(100\) residents in location \(1\text{,}\) \(200\) in location \(2\) and \(400\) in location \(3\text{.}\) Find the population of each location in the long run.
    🔗
    🔗

    11. Long-Run Proportions.

    A migration matrix is given by
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{5} \amp \frac{1}{2} \amp \frac{1}{5} \\[2pt] \frac{1}{4} \amp \frac{1}{4} \amp \frac{1}{2} \\[2pt] \frac{11}{20} \amp \frac{1}{4} \amp \frac{3}{10} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Compare the populations of the three locations in the long run.
    🔗
    🔗

    12. ★ A Bike-Share Network.

    A bike-share company has three stations. Let \(A\) be the matrix whose \((i,j)\)-entry is the probability that a bike rented at station \(j\) is returned to station \(i\text{,}\) and suppose
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.4 \amp 0.2 \amp 0.2 \\ 0.2 \amp 0.4 \amp 0.2 \\ 0.4 \amp 0.4 \amp 0.6 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Initially the bikes are distributed as \(\vec{V}_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 20 \\ 30 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) How are they distributed in the long run?
    🔗
    🔗

    13. ★ A Deer Population.

    A deer population consists of \(40\) fawns and \(40\) adult does. Suppose that
    1. the number of fawns born in any year is twice the number of adult does in the previous year;
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(70\%\) of adult does survive to the next year;
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(30\%\) of fawns survive to become adult does.
      🔗
      🔗
    Will the population survive?
    🔗

    \begin{equation*} 1 \to 2, \quad 1 \to 3, \quad 2 \to 3, \quad 3 \to 1. \end{equation*}
    So page \(1\) links out to pages \(2\) and \(3\text{,}\) page \(2\) links only to page \(3\text{,}\) and page \(3\) links only to page \(1\text{.}\)
