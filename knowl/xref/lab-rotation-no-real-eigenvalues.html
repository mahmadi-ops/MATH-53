Exercise 5. Why a Rotation Has No Real Eigenvalues.

Let \(A\) be the matrix of the linear transformation that rotates every vector in \(\R^2\) counterclockwise through \(\pi/6\text{.}\) Explain, using the geometry of eigenvectors, why \(A\) can have no real eigenvalues.
🔗
Solution.
Suppose \(\lambda \in \R\) were an eigenvalue with eigenvector \(\vec{v} \neq \vec{0}\text{,}\) so \(A\vec{v} = \lambda\vec{v}\text{.}\) Then \(A\vec{v}\) is a scalar multiple of \(\vec{v}\text{,}\) which means it lies on the same line through the origin as \(\vec{v}\text{.}\) That is the geometric content of being an eigenvector, as in Figure 8.8: \(A\) may stretch, shrink, or reverse \(\vec{v}\text{,}\) but it may not turn it off its own line.
🔗
A rotation through \(\pi/6\) turns every nonzero vector by \(30^\circ\text{.}\) Since \(\pi/6\) is not a multiple of \(\pi\text{,}\) no nonzero vector ends up on its original line. So no nonzero \(\vec{v}\) can satisfy \(A\vec{v} = \lambda\vec{v}\) for a real \(\lambda\text{,}\) and \(A\) has no real eigenvalues.
🔗
The two exceptional angles are worth noting: rotation through \(0\) is the identity, for which every nonzero vector is an eigenvector with \(\lambda = 1\text{,}\) and rotation through \(\pi\) reverses every vector, giving \(\lambda = -1\text{.}\)
