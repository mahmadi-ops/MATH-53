Exercise 23. Inverses of Transposes and Products.

  1. Show that if \(A\) is an invertible \(n \times n\) matrix, then so is \(A^T\text{,}\) and \((A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
  2. Show that \((AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}\) by verifying both \(AB(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = I\) and \(B^{-1}A^{-1}(AB) = I\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
  3. Show that \((ABC)^{-1} = C^{-1}B^{-1}A^{-1}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
Throughout you may use that an inverse, when it exists, is unique (Theorem 4.33): if \(MN = I\) and \(NM = I\text{,}\) then \(N = M^{-1}\text{.}\)
🔗
Solution.
(a) We show that \((A^{-1})^T\) behaves like an inverse of \(A^T\text{.}\) Using \(M^T N^T = (NM)^T\text{,}\)
\begin{equation*} A^T (A^{-1})^T = (A^{-1}A)^T = I^T = I, \end{equation*}
\begin{equation*} (A^{-1})^T A^T = (AA^{-1})^T = I^T = I. \end{equation*}
By uniqueness of the inverse, \(A^T\) is invertible with \((A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T\text{.}\)
🔗
(b) Regroup and cancel from the inside out:
\begin{equation*} (AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = AIA^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I, \end{equation*}
\begin{equation*} (B^{-1}A^{-1})(AB) = B^{-1}(A^{-1}A)B = B^{-1}IB = B^{-1}B = I. \end{equation*}
So \(B^{-1}A^{-1}\) acts as the inverse of \(AB\text{,}\) and by uniqueness \((AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}\text{.}\)
🔗
(c) The same peeling, one layer deeper:
\begin{align*} (ABC)(C^{-1}B^{-1}A^{-1}) \amp = AB(CC^{-1})B^{-1}A^{-1}\\ \amp = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I, \end{align*}
\begin{align*} (C^{-1}B^{-1}A^{-1})(ABC) \amp = C^{-1}B^{-1}(A^{-1}A)BC\\ \amp = C^{-1}(B^{-1}B)C = C^{-1}C = I. \end{align*}
Both (b) and (c) are instances of Theorem 4.47: inverting a product reverses the order of the factors, just as transposing one does.
