Example 7.19 The Same Vector in Two Coordinate Systems.

Let \(\mathcal{B}_1 = \{ \vec{e}_1, \vec{e}_2 \}\) be the standard basis of \(\R^2\) and let \(\mathcal{B}_2 = \{ \vec{b}_1, \vec{b}_2 \}\) be a second basis, related to the first by
\begin{equation*} \vec{e}_1 = \tfrac{1}{2}\vec{b}_1, \qquad \vec{e}_2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\vec{b}_1 + \vec{b}_2. \end{equation*}
🔗
A vector \(\vec{x}\) with \([\vec{x}]_{\mathcal{B}_1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) i.e., \(\vec{x} = \vec{e}_1 + 2\vec{e}_2\text{,}\) has
\begin{equation*} [\vec{x}]_{\mathcal{B}_2} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{3}{2} \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \text{i.e.} \qquad \vec{x} = \tfrac{3}{2}\vec{b}_1 + 2\vec{b}_2. \end{equation*}
The vector \(\vec{x}\) has not moved; only the grid we measure it against has changed.
🔗
Solving the two relations above for \(\vec{b}_1\) and \(\vec{b}_2\) gives
\begin{equation*} \vec{b}_1 = 2\vec{e}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \vec{b}_2 = \vec{e}_2 - \vec{e}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
so \(\mathcal{B}_2\) rules the plane with a grid of parallelograms rather than squares.
🔗
Predict before playing. If the grid changes while the tip of \(\vec{x}=(1,2)\) stays fixed, must its coordinate pair stay the same?
🔗
Animate the two coordinate systems. The wine vector stays fixed while the standard grid changes into the grid for \(\vec{b}_1=(2,0)\) and \(\vec{b}_2=(-1,1)\text{.}\) The olive and mustard contributions add head to tail to the same vector at every stage. Use Play, the endpoint buttons, or the slider to compare the two coordinate descriptions.
🔗

To keep a valid basis throughout the motion, at progress \(t\) we use
\begin{equation*} \vec{b}_1(t)=(1+t,0),\qquad \vec{b}_2(t)=(-t,1),\qquad 0\le t\le1. \end{equation*}
The determinant of these two columns is \(1+t>0\text{,}\) so they are always independent. The changing coordinates are
\begin{equation*} [\vec{x}]_{\mathcal{B}(t)}= \begin{bmatrix}(1+2t)/(1+t) \\ 2\end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
Indeed, their scaled arrows sum to
\begin{equation*} \frac{1+2t}{1+t}\vec{b}_1(t)+2\vec{b}_2(t) =(1+2t,0)+(-2t,2)=(1,2). \end{equation*}
At the end this reads \(\vec{x}=\tfrac32\vec{b}_1+2\vec{b}_2\text{:}\) the new coordinates are \((3/2,2)\text{,}\) and the geometric vector has stayed in place.
