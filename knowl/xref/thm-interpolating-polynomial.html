Theorem 1.45 Polynomial Interpolation.

Given \(n\) data points \((x_1,y_1), (x_2,y_2), \ldots, (x_n,y_n)\) with the \(x_i\) distinct, there is a unique polynomial \(p(x) = r_0 + r_1 x + \cdots + r_{n-1}x^{n-1}\) of degree at most \(n - 1\) such that \(p(x_i) = y_i\) for \(i = 1, 2, \ldots, n\text{.}\) The polynomial \(p(x)\) is called the interpolating polynomial for the data points.
🔗
🔗

Proof.
Uniqueness. Suppose \(p(x)\) and \(q(x)\) are two polynomials of degree at most \(n-1\) that both pass through the data. Their difference \(d(x) = p(x) - q(x)\) is again a polynomial of degree at most \(n-1\text{,}\) and \(d(x_i) = y_i - y_i = 0\) for each of the \(n\) distinct numbers \(x_1, \ldots, x_n\text{.}\) But a nonzero polynomial of degree at most \(n-1\) has at most \(n-1\) roots. So \(d(x)\) is the zero polynomial, that is, \(p(x) = q(x)\text{.}\)
🔗
Existence. We postpone this half. It will follow from uniqueness once we have the Invertible Matrix Theorem (Section 4.6): for a system with as many equations as unknowns, if the solution is unique whenever one exists, then one always exists.
